The existence of a path does not yet tell us how to calculate along it. The first sum of positions multiplied by increments looked like an extension of ordinary integration, yet left and right endpoints left different answers. The difference was not insufficient precision. The choice of observation time and the accumulation of squared changes had both been hidden inside the assertion that the error would vanish.

In rebuilding integration, the order of information became part of the definition. Predictability does not reveal the next change; it restricts which choices may already participate in its accumulation. Conditional orthogonality, an isometry and completeness extend finite choices into processes. An integral therefore cannot be specified by a completed curve alone. We must also state how information unfolds, which measure controls error, and in what sense the approximations converge.

Second-order variation became more than an extra term in a chain rule. The quadratic clock of a continuous martingale connects pathwise refinement with conditional compensation. Actual jumps require the entire change of a function to be retained: a fixed jump cannot be discarded as a higher-order remainder. Itô and symmetric integration can describe the same motion while placing common variation in different parts of the formula. Changing notation requires the other terms of an equation to change with it.

Local time gave accumulation another meaning. Ordinary residence time at a point can vanish while contact has a nontrivial intensity. That intensity can organize excursions, build new clocks and enter equations with singular sources. Ordinary time, quadratic clocks and spatial occupation densities retain different information. Choosing the right measure does not turn zero into something nonzero; it identifies which question the original zero actually answered.

Calculation must also pass a test of existence. A stochastic equation specifies a rule, rather than an already constructed motion. Uniqueness of paths for a given noise, uniqueness of probability laws, and existence for all time require distinct proofs. Changing probability weights can change drift without adding information. Studying responses in noise directions does not give a Brownian path a time derivative. These distinctions let us ask more precise questions instead of collecting every possibility under a single phrase such as a stochastic solution.

On a curved space, even the drift in an equation cannot be interpreted in isolation. The inward correction on a circle comes from a second-order coordinate transformation; it does not mean that the particle leaves the circle. What persists is the compatible mathematical relation among different observations. Coordinates, connections, metrics and boundary rules can now be distinguished: some changes merely alter a record, while others genuinely alter the motion.

The calculus obtained in this volume is consequently more than a collection of new formulas. It brings previously implicit assumptions into view: what is known before a choice, which changes may be neglected, which sources permit classical solutions, and which local rules determine paths. The new concepts do not revoke classical mathematics. They identify its assumptions and establish verifiable calculations for objects it did not previously accommodate.

Knowing how motion exists and accumulates still does not determine what a long observation will reveal. Each spherical path stays on the sphere, although its mean position can move toward the centre; local symmetry has not immediately identified a path's long-term average. A further question remains. Even when records depend on one another, can invariance of their joint law under time shifts make their averages settle? If they settle, can some random information survive the averaging?

Calculus has made the structure of changes along a path readable. We next need to identify what a probability law retains under repeated observation and rearrangement.
