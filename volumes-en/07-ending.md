Two experiments may leave equally many points, even with the same mean count in every window, without producing the same spatial randomness. What was missing was how locations occur together in one realization. Studying that difference required more than additional numbers: the whole outcome first had to become an object capable of carrying joint probabilities.

The question of when records really realize the claimed object recurred throughout the constructions. Window counts had to be simultaneously countably additive. Hit records had to exclude negative joint probabilities and realize limit points in shrinking windows. Offspring strings had to exhaust pending visits only at the end. These are different consistency conditions, but each prevents individually plausible readings from being assembled into a complete random world without proof.

Some properties that had seemed intrinsic revealed themselves as additional conventions. Dropping unit mass allowed finite particles to approach diffuse mass; forgetting mass could leave a closed shape. But discarding multiplicity did not preserve counts, and preserving population did not preserve ancestors. Conversely, a precisely chosen traversal retained branching and tree distance in one path. A shorter record need not lose information when a recovery theorem actually establishes what it retains.

Probability itself had levels that could not be collapsed. Mass supported on lines in each realization could average to planar area. Selecting an experiment and selecting an occurrence could weight the same environment differently. Joint Laplace readings identified a random measure’s law; Campbell disintegration identified environments selected by mass. Both answered questions beyond a mean density, as the different Palm calculations for Poisson backgrounds, random intensities and cluster companions made concrete.

Limits tested what had actually been retained. Local integrals could miss escaping mass, a persistent location could carry vanishing weight, and simple points could merge into multiplicity. Closeness of measures therefore did not automatically imply closeness of supports; similarity of pictures could not replace similarity of genealogies. Each state space required its own account of which errors disappear and which contacts or relations must remain, followed by proof that the limit still belongs to that space.

Conditioned large trees made this requirement especially sharp. Finite trees were already exactly recoverable, yet fixing total size constrained formerly independent offspring records. Bridge weights, minimum cuts and ancestral-line control addressed rare selection, stability of operations and hidden deep branches respectively. Only together did they establish a joint continuous-excursion limit. Encodability and stable passage of an encoding to a limit became two conclusions requiring different proofs.

The volume has therefore done more than enlarge a list of random objects. We have gained ways to judge whether a proposed random outcome exists, which joint observations identify it and which conditions let it pass to a limit. Measurability, construction, identification and convergence did not replace one another. Testing them anew for new objects made previously hidden assumptions identifiable, changeable and open to counterexamples.

The continuous excursion now in hand is still a real function indexed by time. Minima recorded common ancestors in discrete trees. Could they define genuine distances on a continuous curve as well? If different times pointed to the same ancestor, what should be identified? Answering this would make a continuous code into a tree, rather than merely a curve associated with one.
