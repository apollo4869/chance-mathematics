One ancestor can be visited twice by a contour, and one random-tree law can arise from different experiments. Both kinds of “sameness” are easily replaced by pictures at first, but eventually require different arguments. The first identifies times at zero distance and proves that the quotient has unique connecting arcs. The second compares probability laws, establishing when excursions, conditioned families and sequential growth produce the same rooted object with distance and mass.

A continuous curve does not acquire ancestry merely by being called a tree contour. Only after interval minima enter the distance formula do the triangle inequality, cutpoints and unique arcs turn that relation into geometry. Nor does time directly become a position on the tree: distinct times may represent one point, while uniform time sampling leaves a pushforward mass. Changing traversal speed can preserve distances and change that mass. Shape and sampling have thus been placed rigorously in one object without becoming the same datum.

Comparing such objects no longer requires a common sheet of drawing paper. A correspondence allows different numbers of points while controlling pairwise distance distortion. A coupling allows a little mismatched mass but must remain compatible with the geometric comparison. A long branch almost never selected by samples is still a genuine long branch of the whole space. It separates closeness seen by finite samples from closeness of the entire tree.

The spatial limit of conditioned large trees therefore goes beyond a curve limit. Contour errors must become distance errors, first-visit clocks must align with continuous time, and uniform vertex mass must be coupled with excursion-time mass. Only when these errors vanish together do the root, distances and mass survive simultaneously. The common continuum limit does not remove conditioning or finite-variance hypotheses. It explains how those conditions make certain microscopic differences disappear from the structure being compared.

Allowing a tree to change over time requires further choices. A hierarchy records containment but not splitting clocks; exchangeability does not ensure mass conservation; conservation at each local split does not exclude infinitely many accelerated splits producing dust in finite time. The same static tree admits different cutting rules. Dynamics is not a unique answer already hidden in shape. It requires additional, mutually consistent probability rules.

Sequential branch growth faces the same demand in reverse. Running every finite step does not yet construct an infinite random tree. Closing the Brownian line-breaking construction uses finite densities to identify the whole growth record, compactness to control all future branches, and a separate proof that empirical leaf mass exists. Total skeleton length may be infinite while the tree stays compact. Mass may be carried by leaves while supporting the whole tree. These distinct accumulations and limits cannot be replaced by a single appeal to infinity.

Finally, the Brownian limit of fixed-k-ary families shows that continuum geometry need not preserve discrete offspring counts literally. Published edge-growth models, stable genealogies and persistent hubs show that other mechanisms can retain different structures. Comparing them requires their own models, scales and sampling conditions. The established Brownian theory and the restricted constructions proved here do not also complete general Lévy height, Ray–Knight, heavy-tailed conditioned-tree theory or every multifurcating model. Their classical sources can be checked; the book’s missing arguments must remain distinct from its proved results.

A random tree is now more than a visible shape. We can specify how its points are identified, how distances agree, how mass is sampled, how local rules form an infinite object and what a limit preserves. This understanding also gives new models definite tests: rather than naming a shape and expecting properties to follow, we make construction, probability law, sampling and limits meet under stated conditions.
