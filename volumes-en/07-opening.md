Record several arrivals on a sheet and a collection of points appears along a time axis. Record where the events occurred as well, and the marks spread through space. Reporting only their total number now discards a central part of the experiment: the same ten points may crowd into one corner or remain far apart.

Numbering the points seems to recover their locations. Yet one experiment may produce three points and the next seven, while changing their numbering should not change the outcome. If two events occur at the same location, drawing a single dot would also collapse two occurrences into one. A useful record must accommodate a changing count, retain positions and multiplicities, and avoid mistaking artificial labels for experimental information.

Counting points in a finite window is a direct observation. Moving or shrinking the window, or weighting locations differently, gives further readings. Which readings jointly determine the entire random outcome? If infinitely many points remain outside, which limits can still be observed locally? These questions require a measurable state space for a collection of points, rather than simply transplanting a Poisson count formula into a spatial picture.

Recording one unit of mass at each occurrence puts counting and location into a single measure. Allowing noninteger mass would extend this language to diffuse mass, clusters and random distributions themselves. Observation rules still cannot be freely interchanged: looking from a fixed location and selecting an existing point before looking around can produce different typical environments. Such observations need a probability construction, not division by the probability-zero event that a point occurs exactly here.

Locations may also form a set; preserving ancestry between them adds the geometry of a tree. Forgetting counts, multiplicities or kinship are different simplifications. Each record retains answers to some questions while making others inaccessible. The state space and the notion of convergence will therefore be part of the mathematics itself.

This volume starts with the concrete outcome of a collection of random locations. We first make counts, measurability and spatial observations compatible, then ask which structures can still be identified and passed to a limit as the objects expand. Connections among points, mass, sets and continuous shapes require their own constructions and proofs. Being drawable in space does not make them the same random object.
