A traversal curve may return to the same branch point twice. Time has passed, but position has not changed. Conversely, equal heights may belong to entirely different branches. Treating time as position stretches the tree into a line; treating height alone as position collapses different branches at the same level.

In a discrete contour, the common ancestor height lies in the minimum between two visits. The continuous excursion limit still supplies that number, but no longer comes with vertices and edges. Turning it into geometry first requires deciding which times represent one point, then proving that the resulting distances are consistent and introduce no loops.

Nor can similarity of drawings stand for closeness of trees. Two trees need not inhabit a common coordinate space or have equally many branches. Even with similar lengths, their mass may sit differently. Distances, roots and sampling mass record different things. Comparing them together requires mathematical definitions, rather than an increasingly smooth picture.

Finite sampling offers something concrete to test. Select a few descendants and keep only the branches connecting them to the root. Can their branching shape and edge lengths be calculated? How should these small trees agree as the sample grows? An infinite random geometry must support such finite observations, without those observations being mistaken for a complete spatial limit theorem.

Cutting a branch, merging families or attaching new branches will likewise require rules not supplied by a static geometry. Compatibility of mass, branching mechanisms that preserve limits and local growth that constructs a whole space must each be established under the stated models and assumptions; public results from broader theory cannot replace arguments not yet completed here. A continuous shape is therefore not an end to the exploration. It permits questions that population counts and path readings alone could not yet state precisely.
