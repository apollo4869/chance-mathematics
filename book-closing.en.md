# The Mathematics of Chance: From Answers to the Conditions of Judgment

> Working draft of the book’s intellectual closing, for author review. Separate from the eight volumes and 58 chapters, this is neither an added chapter nor an approved publication epilogue. It does not certify closure of all proofs or completion of independent expert review.

Returning to the unfinished division of stakes, we can now ask more precisely how the money should be shared: what are the rules, what information is already available, and which future is being averaged? A justified judgment need not wait for the outcome. Its grounds, however, must not disappear behind a calculated number. The initial problem of fairness brought the future into calculation; the later exploration has taught us to specify what that calculation actually applies to.

This ability first changes our expectations of regularity. Long-run averages can stabilize while different records retain different limiting values. Under measure preservation, stationarity and integrability, the ergodic theorem identifies the limit with a conditional average over invariant information. Only suitable ergodicity makes it the same deterministic constant. Averaging away local fluctuations and eliminating all randomness are distinct conclusions. Dependence is not the opposite of long-run regularity, and independence is not the only source of stability.

These distinctions cannot stop at calling observations correlated or uncorrelated. An invariant probability need not attract every initial state; ergodicity does not automatically remove periodicity. Forgetting requires additional coupling, regeneration and aperiodicity conditions. Conversely, invariance under finite permutations may retain a shared random directing probability, while arrays organize dependence through shared indices. Symmetry becomes a testable restriction rather than a synonym for being sufficiently uniform. We can identify which symmetry a model supplies and what it leaves undetermined.

Understanding probability also requires understanding selection. The environment at a fixed location and the environment seen from a typical occurrence may have different laws; high-intensity environments are more likely to be selected by events. Campbell measures and Palm disintegration express this weighting as a rigorous conditional structure. No observer has altered the original system, and there is no universally applicable typical object. Sampling is part of the experiment and must be declared and tested like its generating rules.

Infinity changes how those declarations work. Almost sure validity at every preselected time is not simultaneous validity at all times of a path. Zero ordinary occupation time at a fixed position does not exclude nonzero local time there. This separates quantifiers, null sets and measures rather than dismissing experience through abstraction. Countable determining classes, common null sets, continuous versions and occupation densities give provable meanings to all, almost everywhere and no time spent.

Limits further train that judgment. Many finite-variance microscopic models can yield the same Brownian structure, and fixed-k-ary families need not retain offspring counts literally in their continuum trees. Changing tails, conditioning or scales may change the limit. Universality does not make assumptions dispensable. It reveals which differences cease to affect the structure under precise conditions, and which differences still determine the answer.

Proving such preservation requires a comparison first. Finite-dimensional readings may converge while paths become uncontrolled between observations. Finite samples may almost miss a long branch while the full tree retains its extra distance. Tightness and mass couplings do real work at these points. Topology is not a final choice of notation: it specifies which perturbations may be ignored and requires limits to remain in the stated object space.

The rate at which probability vanishes raises another comparison. Ordinary weak convergence cannot alone identify the exponential cost of rare events; large deviations need another level of tightness and approximation control. The squared energy of small-noise paths then has a precise role, without giving typical Brownian paths integrable velocities. The same functional expression can serve different purposes on different probability scales. Mathematics discovers those connections while requiring that we not turn them into unconditional identities.

When outcomes become paths, measures, closed sets or geometric spaces, new language does more than lengthen notation. Window counts must jointly form a measure, hitting probabilities must satisfy the consistency conditions of a closed shape, and a coding curve must yield a quotient without extra loops. Laplace functionals, capacities and sampled distance arrays have different identifying powers. A mean is not an entire probability law, and consistency of finite readings need not construct the intended random object.

Construction, uniqueness, identifiability and stability under limits thus become separate tasks. A stochastic equation gives local rules without yet proving existence. Pathwise uniqueness given the noise differs from uniqueness in law. The same interior generator expression may describe different motions under different boundary rules. A continuous excursion can construct a real tree, but it takes another proof to transfer curve errors into geometry and mass errors. These results are not instances of one universal theorem; together they prevent one success from being mistaken for completion of every task.

Calculation itself faces the same scrutiny. Accumulating Brownian squared increments forces a second-order contribution into the chain rule; actual jumps require full function differences. Predictability specifies which choices may already enter an integral when the change arrives. Itô and symmetric integrals use different conventions but can describe the same motion after corresponding corrections. On manifolds, correct second-order transformations make coordinate formulas compatible. Classical calculus has not universally failed: the regularity that once controlled discarded errors is absent, and new paths and information conditions require new arguments.

These theories have not made the unknown future known. Reweighting probabilities may change drift without adding information. A Markov state retains the past information useful for prediction without asserting that the world has no history. Differentiability in noise directions does not supply a time derivative to sample paths. These structures are powerful because they permit precise calculation while preventing interpretations from extending beyond the theorem.

Nor is this one discovery history shared by all probability theory. Gambling, statistical inference, random motion, symmetry and random geometry have different origins. New tools do not arise only from the failure of old ones. An established representation may suggest a reverse construction; an additional constraint may transform one random object into another. What transfers between problems is the ability to find structure and test arguments, not a fixed plot that dispenses with the mathematics.

At this stage of the manuscript, three things must still be separated: restricted proofs written within the book, classical conclusions with public sources, and arguments not yet completed here. General Lévy height, Ray–Knight, heavy-tailed conditioned trees and broader model classes retain their proof obligations even when the book has a closing. Written proofs also still require independent mathematical review. Marking these boundaries does not add another theorem; it preserves the grounds each claim actually has.

What we take away is therefore more than a collection of answers. For a new random phenomenon, we can identify the object and information to preserve, then choose observations that can identify it. For a valid limit, we can trace the source of uniform control. For a failed inference, we can name the missing condition rather than blame an indistinct chance. Mathematics lets us calculate randomness and makes our judgments themselves objects of analysis, revision and further development. Recognizing unanswered questions within proved relations is where this exploration can continue.
