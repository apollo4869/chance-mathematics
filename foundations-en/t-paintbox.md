# T-PAINTBOX: Exchangeable Partitions, Frequencies and Unique Mixtures

This proof concerns partitions of N, without assuming finitely many blocks or absence of dust. Its tools are countable constructions from Chapter 8, the conditional strong law from Chapter 12, and standard Borel de Finetti and probability kernels from Chapter 43. Return to [Chapter 47](../chapters/47-partitions.md#partitions-paintbox).

## 1. A genuine measurable space for partitions

<a id="paintbox-proof-space"></a>

Record a partition by its zero-one equivalence matrix. Reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity each impose conditions on finitely many coordinates, so the space P∞ is a closed subset of the countable cube. Its restriction rₙ takes values in the finite space Pₙ. Thus P∞ is compact metrizable and standard Borel, with Borel field generated by finite restrictions. Ordering blocks by their least members is a measurable enumeration convention, not a decreasing-size order.

Exchangeability means that every finite relabeling preserves the law of the entire partition. Consistent exchangeable laws on Pₙ give a unique infinite exchangeable law: construct the countable sequence of restrictions by Chapter 8; all adjacent consistency events hold on one probability-one set and determine a unique equivalence relation. Invariance extends from cylinders. A single finite exchangeable law need not admit such an infinite consistent family.

## 2. What independent block labels preserve

<a id="paintbox-proof-labels"></a>

On an independent product extension, attach an iid Uniform(0,1) label Zⱼ to each block in least-member order, and let Xᵢ be its block label. Block enumeration is measurable: whether i is the jth least representative depends only on the first i relations. Hence Xᵢ is measurable. Countably many independent continuous labels are almost surely distinct, so the original partition is recovered exactly by equality of labels.

Given the partition, the conditional law of finitely many X values depends only on their equivalence relation: one uniform per block, independent across blocks. Relabeling can change discovery order but not this kernel. Integrating against the exchangeable partition proves that X is an infinite exchangeable sequence. Chapter 43 therefore supplies a measurable random probability μ whose countable product is the conditional law of X. This does not assume conditionally iid edges in a general exchangeable graph.

\[ \Pi=\operatorname{Eq}(X),\qquad \mathcal L(X\mid\mu)=\mu^{\otimes\mathbb N}. \]

## 3. All block frequencies on one common set

<a id="paintbox-proof-frequencies"></a>

Fix μ. Conditional on Xᵢ=x, the other indicators of equality with x are iid with success probability μ({x}). The strong law, integrated over x, gives the frequency of the block of i. Intersect over countably many i. Positive atoms are countable, and each is sampled almost surely, since the probability of missing it forever is zero. Thus the positive block frequencies are exactly all atomic masses of μ, with multiplicity.

\[ F_i=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n\mathbf1_{\{i\sim j\}}=\mu(\{X_i\})\quad\text{a.s. simultaneously for all }i. \]

The evaluation μ({x}) is jointly measurable, either through decreasing grid neighborhoods or kernel integration of the measurable diagonal. For fixed distinct i,j, the probability of equality at a zero-mass point is the integral of μ({x}) over the zero-mass points against μ, which is zero. A countable intersection shows that every zero-frequency block is a singleton. Such singletons may have positive aggregate density, but cannot form an infinite zero-frequency block.

Let Jᵢ indicate that i is its block’s least member. The numbers bᵢ=JᵢFᵢ record each frequency once. Finite sums of disjoint block frequencies are at most one, hence their countable sum is at most one. The strong law for draws in the nonatomic part shows that the density of zero-frequency singletons is d=1−Σbᵢ. Adding individual zero frequencies is not a valid way to deny this positive mass.

## 4. Ranking, measurable parameters and the paintbox kernel

<a id="paintbox-proof-kernel"></a>

On the Borel event where frequencies exist, rank b in decreasing order to obtain s; set s to zero on the bad event. For q>0 at most 1/q entries exceed q. The jth largest value has the following countable-supremum expression, with empty supremum zero. This proves measurability without choosing unique labels for tied masses.

\[ s_j=\sup\{q\in\mathbb Q\cap(0,1):\sum_i\mathbf1_{\{b_i>q\}}\ge j\},\qquad \Delta_\downarrow=\{s_1\ge s_2\ge\cdots\ge0:\sum_i s_i\le1\}. \]

The ranked simplex is closed in the countable cube, using ordering and finite partial-sum constraints. For s, draw iid uniforms Uᵢ. Consecutive intervals of lengths sⱼ specify positive-mass blocks. Every draw in the remaining interval forms its own singleton, rather than one common dust block. Finite equality patterns are Borel functions of (s,U). Their laws Qₛ form a probability kernel on P∞, by measurable cylinder evaluations followed by a monotone-class argument.

## 5. The exact finite-pattern formula, including dust

<a id="paintbox-proof-eppf"></a>

Fix a particular partition π of [n] with block sizes n₁,…,nₖ, and let J₁ index its singleton blocks. Choose D⊂J₁ for the blocks produced by dust. All remaining blocks must fall in distinct positive-mass intervals. Independent sampling and nonnegative countable summation give the formula below. It is the probability of a specified partition, without an additional block-order factor.

\[ p_s(n_1,\ldots,n_k)=\sum_{D\subseteq J_1}d^{|D|}\!\sum_{\substack{j_\ell\ge1\ (\ell\notin D)\\j_\ell\ \text{all distinct}}}\prod_{\ell\notin D}s_{j_\ell}^{\,n_\ell},\qquad d=1-\sum_j s_j. \]

Empty products and the inner sum with no nondust blocks are one; d⁰=1. All terms are nonnegative and sum to at most one, since they describe disjoint sampling events. Thus measurability does not require exchanging signed infinite sums. The expression is symmetric in block sizes. A mixed partition has EPPF E \(p_S\).

## 6. Representation and uniqueness, not just a candidate formula

<a id="paintbox-proof-representation"></a>

For fixed μ, the conditional strong law identifies the ranked frequencies of Eq(X) with its ranked atomic masses; finite pattern probabilities depend only on these masses and the remaining nonatomic mass. Measurable dependence need not use a measurable selection of atoms: define hⱼ(μ) as the integral of Sⱼ(Eq(x)) under the countable product of μ. The product kernel is measurable, and for each fixed μ the strong law identifies this integral with the jth largest atomic mass. This gives a Borel mass code for every μ.

Hence S=h(μ) almost surely on the labeled extension. Conditional on μ, finite pattern probabilities are \(p_{h(\mu)}\). The tower property conditional on S gives the cylinder identities for \(Q_S\). A countable determining class allows one common null set, after which probability uniqueness identifies the entire conditional law. Integrating out the auxiliary labels gives the representation of the original partition; S itself was already a measurable function of that partition.

\[ \mathcal L(\Pi\mid S)=Q_S,\qquad \mathcal L(\Pi)=\int_{\Delta_\downarrow}Q_s\,\nu(ds),\qquad \nu=\mathcal L(S). \]

Conversely every paintbox is exchangeable. For each fixed s, the independent strong law on one countable common set recovers exactly s as its ranked frequencies. Any other mixing measure producing the same partition law must therefore have the same pushforward under the measurable frequency map, so it equals ν. What is unique is the mixing law of ranked masses, not colors, interval locations or discovery-order names.

## 7. Size bias in discovery order and finite observations

<a id="paintbox-proof-order"></a>

Given s, the first individual belongs to positive block j with probability sⱼ, or to a dust singleton with probability d. Thus the first discovered block is size-biased, not the largest. Without dust, after some positive blocks have been discovered, the next new block is selected in proportion to the remaining masses: wait until an independent draw lands in their union, then condition on that event. With dust, new singletons keep intervening, so the dust-free permutation description cannot be copied unchanged.

Almost-sure frequency identification uses the entire infinite partition. A fixed sample of n singletons could come from pure dust or from many tiny positive blocks, and does not identify d=1. Infinitely many atoms do not imply dust: masses 2⁻ʲ sum to one. Exchangeability matters: the deterministic partition putting all squares into one block and leaving the other integers singleton has an infinite zero-density block, but is not exchangeable.

Even without dust small samples can fail to distinguish masses: s=(1/2,1/2) and t=(2/3,1/6,1/6) both have two-person collision probability 1/2, but three-person single-block probabilities 1/4 and 11/36. Their complete two-label partition laws agree despite different ranked masses; a third label supplies genuinely new information.
