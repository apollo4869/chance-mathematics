# T-HIERARCHY-CONSISTENCY: Finite Restrictions, Nested Paintboxes and the Scope of Tree Representation

This page proves the hierarchy constructions in [Chapter 56](../chapters/56-fragmentation-hierarchies.md#hierarchy-space). Prerequisites are countable probability construction from Chapter 8, standard Borel de Finetti from Chapter 43, paintboxes from Chapter 47 and ancestral distances from Chapter 54. The full IP-tree representation classification of arbitrary unclocked exchangeable hierarchies is not proved here. We prove finite hierarchies, nested partitions at countably many specified levels, and measured real-tree representation when each level is dust-free.

## 1. Hierarchies as compatible finite observations

<a id="hierarchy-projective"></a>

A hierarchy H on [n] contains the empty set, [n] and every singleton; intersecting members must be nested. Its restriction rₘH={A∩[m]:A∈H} has the same properties. The space Hₙ of such families is finite and discrete. Define an infinite hierarchy as a compatible sequence (Hₙ), rather than an unrestricted random subfamily of 2ᴺ. Compatibility is closed in a countable product of finite discrete spaces. The inverse limit is compact Polish and its Borel field is generated by finite restrictions.

If (rₙ)₊pₙ₊₁=pₙ, define the law of finitely many restriction coordinates by taking the largest coordinate and projecting down. These finite-dimensional laws are consistent. Chapter 8 constructs their countable-product probability; the countably many adjacent compatibility events have probability 1, so it is supported on the inverse limit. Cylinder uniqueness proves uniqueness. If every pₙ is invariant under relabeling [n], use a sufficiently large finite coordinate to verify invariance on each cylinder under a fixed finite permutation, then extend to the Borel field. This constructs an infinite exchangeable hierarchy.

## 2. Finite tree representation does not supply consistent lengths

<a id="hierarchy-finite-tree"></a>

For each nonempty member A other than [n], its strict supersets are totally ordered and have a unique smallest member, parent(A). Connect A to that parent. Strict inclusion excludes cycles and every node reaches the root. Singletons are leaves. Maximal proper children of an internal node are disjoint and cover it because all singletons are present, so there are at least two. Conversely, descendant-label sets in a rooted leaf-labeled tree give a hierarchy. The constructions are inverse for trees with no planar order, no edge lengths and no redundant unary nodes.

Restriction deletes unobserved leaves and suppresses redundant unary nodes. Arbitrarily assigned unit edge lengths are not preserved. In the three-leaf hierarchy with cluster {1,2}, leaves 1 and 3 are three unit edges apart. After restricting to 1,3 and assigning fresh unit lengths, their distance is 2. Topological consistency is not metric consistency after such reassignment.

## 3. How clocked nested partitions produce a hierarchy

<a id="hierarchy-clocked"></a>

Let Πₜ refine without merging, with right-continuous finite restrictions. On [n], collect blocks at all nonnegative rational times and add the root, singletons and empty set, obtaining Hₙ. Each effective change increases block count, so at most n−1 such changes occur. Any nonsingleton block present at a real time persists at some later rational time by right continuity of the finite restriction. Thus rational times omit no finitely visible cluster. Restriction commutes with collecting these blocks and adding singletons, producing an infinite hierarchy.

The hierarchy forgets splitting times and even the order of splits on different branches. Waiting laws cannot be recovered from it. Conversely an arbitrary compatible hierarchy does not already carry a right-continuous Markov clock. This construction does not claim that every hierarchy already has fragmentation dynamics.

## 4. One directing probability couples all specified levels

<a id="hierarchy-nested-paintbox"></a>

Theorem: for jointly exchangeable partitions Π⁽ᵐ⁾, m=1,2,…, refining with m, there is a random Q∈P([0,1]ᴺ) such that iid rows Xᵢ conditional on Q generate their joint law by equality of the m-th coordinates. For the proof enumerate each level’s blocks by least member and attach independent continuous uniform labels Zₘ,ⱼ. Set Xᵢ(m) to its block label. Countably many labels are almost surely distinct. Every finite row-and-level labeling law depends only on the original hierarchy relations, so Xᵢ is an exchangeable sequence in [0,1]ᴺ. Chapter 43 gives measurable directing Q and conditional iid rows. Coordinate equalities recover all level relations, hence all joint restrictions.

\[ i\sim_{\Pi^{(m)}}j\ \Longleftrightarrow\
X_i(m)=X_j(m),\qquad
Q^{\otimes2}\{x_m=y_m,\ x_\ell\ne y_\ell\}=0
\quad(\ell<m). \]

The last condition follows from almost sure nesting and conditional expectation, with one null set for countably many level pairs. It also suffices for the converse: given such Q, intersect the good events for countably many sample and level pairs to obtain simultaneous nesting. Each level’s paintbox frequencies are the ranked atom masses of the corresponding Q marginal; its nonatomic part is dust. The strong law holds jointly over countably many levels and atoms. Auxiliary Q is not unique: relabeling level colors leaves partitions unchanged. Their coordinate names are not identifiable.

## 5. Equal frequencies at each level need not give equal nesting

<a id="hierarchy-marginals"></a>

Take level frequencies (.6,.4) and (.4,.3,.2,.1). Model A puts masses.4,.2 in the first parent and .3,.1 in the second. Model B puts.3,.2,.1 in the first parent and only.4 in the second. The ranked vectors at both levels agree, but the probability that three samples have the same parent and three distinct children is 0 in A and 6×.3×.2×.1=.036 in B. Separately specifying level paintboxes, even with all marginal laws correct, does not specify the hierarchy law.

## 6. Constructing a measured real tree when levels are dust-free

<a id="hierarchy-tree"></a>

Add the precise hypothesis that every finite-level marginal Qₘ is purely atomic. Fix a good Q. Each level has countably many positive-mass colors, and each child color has a unique parent: otherwise two iid samples in that child atom have positive probability of different parents, contradicting the Q² condition. Use these colors as vertices, add a root, connect parents and children and place level m at height aₘ=1−2⁻ᵐ. Equip the union of segments with tree distance and complete it to obtain a separable real tree.

One must not simply assert that completion remains a tree. Heights of a Cauchy sequence converge to h≤1. At any fixed level strictly below h its ancestors eventually agree, since disagreement gives a positive distance lower bound. If h<1, it converges on an existing segment after finitely many levels; if h=1, the stabilized ancestors define an infinite address and its endpoint. The longest common address determines the common ancestor of two points. Root lines are isometrically parametrized by height, giving their geodesic through that ancestor. Deleting an interior point separates prefix branches, so every continuous connecting arc must pass it and is unique.

\[ d_T(\partial x,\partial y)
=2(1-a_{L(x,y)})=2^{1-L(x,y)},\qquad
L(x,y)=\sup\{m:x_m=y_m\},\quad 2^{-\infty}=0. \]

For Q-almost every address, every level lies in a positive atom. The successive vertices converge to a boundary point ∂x. This map is measurable because finite-level choices are measurable and the target metric is complete. Push Q forward to obtain Borel probability μ; the fringe mass of a level vertex equals its atom mass. Iid μ samples recover all nested partitions. Edge-interior points add no clusters. Permanently equal addresses also form a cluster at some sufficiently deep level in each finite sample; singletons are included separately by definition. All finite hierarchy restrictions therefore agree. If every level has only finitely many positive blocks, finite-depth nets make the tree compact. If some level has infinitely many positive blocks, boundary points in them are separated by a fixed positive distance, so it is not compact.

## 7. Dust and general unclocked hierarchies: the actual boundary

<a id="hierarchy-scope"></a>

If the first level is entirely dust, the fixed-height construction puts every distinct sample pair at distance 2. Chapter 55 proved that this cannot be an iid distance law in a separable metric probability space. An uncountable positive-length star must not be silently called a Polish probability tree. The nested-paintbox theorem still holds: coordinate equality on [0,1]ᴺ does not assert that this prefix distance is separably compatible. General exchangeable-hierarchy tree representation needs a different height construction and mass-structure equivalence theory; it is not proved here or replaced by the preceding scoped theorem.

Random-tree measurability in the compact case can also be checked constructively. When each level has finitely many positive blocks, take auxiliary conditionally iid Q addresses. The root span of k samples truncated at level m has only finitely many combinatorial forms, fixed heights and empirical weights 1/k, so is a GHP-valued Borel random element. As k grows, all finite-level atoms are hit and empirical masses converge, giving the entire level-m tree in GHP. As m grows, projecting leaves to level-m ancestors in the same tree bounds Hausdorff and coupled-mass distances by 1−aₘ. The two metric limits give a measurable random compact tree. In the noncompact dust-free case, measurable root and iid-boundary distance readings give a support-plus-root random GP object by Chapter 55; it is not called a compact GHP object.

Even when a tree representation exists, the hierarchy does not identify GHP geometry. Put all mass at the leaf of a single rooted edge. Whether its length is 1 or 2, the sampling hierarchy has only the whole class and singletons, while root-to-sample distance differs. A hierarchy supplies neither a splitting clock, a metric nor Markov dynamics.
