# Chapter 35: Does a Stochastic Differential Equation Have a Solution? — Existence, Uniqueness and the Martingale Problem

Even after a rule of motion has been written as an integral equation, the unknown remains inside the integrals. The next disturbance depends on the present position, which itself depends on all earlier disturbances. Knowing how to integrate a given process does not produce the process that has yet to be constructed.

## 35.1 One Rule Can Permit Different Motions

<a id="sde-rule"></a>

Temporarily set the noise to zero. Starting at the origin, require velocity to be twice the square root of position. Remaining still obeys the rule. So does waiting for any chosen duration and then departing along a parabola.

\[ x(t)=\int_0^t2\sqrt{|x(s)|}\,ds,\qquad x^{(a)}(t)=(t-a)_+^2,\quad a\in[0,\infty],\quad x^{(\infty)}=0. \]

Both one-sided derivatives vanish at departure, so these are not spurious solutions created by gluing. The difficulty is that velocity changes too rapidly with position: moving a small distance from zero does not change velocity by a bounded multiple of that distance. Departure is allowed, but its time is not determined.

At the other extreme, velocity equal to position squared produces finite-time escape from a positive starting point. The expression is legitimate and local motion is unique; global lifetime is missing. These noiseless examples already distinguish writing a rule, existence, uniqueness and existence for all time.

\[ dx_t=x_t^2dt,\quad x_0>0:\qquad x_t=\frac{x_0}{1-x_0t},\quad \zeta=x_0^{-1}. \]

## 35.2 A Solution Must Respect the Order of Information

<a id="sde-notions"></a>

Fix a usual filtration, an m-dimensional Brownian motion W relative to it, and a d-dimensional initial variable ξ measurable at time zero. Consider the following identity, initially for deterministic Borel coefficients. A solution is continuous and adapted, its integrals exist on each finite horizon, and the identity holds for all times outside one common null set.

\[ X_t=\xi+\int_0^t b(s,X_s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(s,X_s)\,dW_s,\qquad \int_0^T\bigl(|b(s,X_s)|+\|\sigma(s,X_s)\|^2\bigr)ds<\infty\quad\text{a.s.} \]

Continuity and adaptation make the state input predictable. Brownian increments must remain independent of the entire past filtration; information obtained by looking ahead cannot be inserted for free. The initial value is consequently independent of future Brownian increments. Matrix norms here are Hilbert–Schmidt norms.

A strong solution is constructed in the usual augmented filtration generated by the given ξ and W. A weak solution may choose the probability space, filtration, initial variable and driver together, while preserving the prescribed initial law and the identity. Weak does not mean approximately solving the equation; it relaxes the requirement that noise and information space be fixed in advance.

Uniqueness also asks two questions. Pathwise uniqueness compares solutions with the same initial variable and driver on the same space and asks for indistinguishability. Uniqueness in law allows different spaces and compares the path laws of X. It does not compare individual trajectories and cannot by itself identify two solutions point by point.

## 35.3 Feeding an Approximation Back into the Rule

<a id="sde-picard"></a>

An unknown on the right does not prevent us from inserting a first guess. Begin with the constant path ξ, evaluate the right side and feed the resulting path back in. This resembles ordinary successive approximation, but the error is now a random process; a Brownian integral cannot be estimated pathwise as a bounded-variation integral.

\[ X^0_t=\xi,\qquad X^{n+1}_t=\xi+\int_0^t b(s,X^n_s)ds+\int_0^t\sigma(s,X^n_s)dW_s. \]

On each finite horizon assume global Lipschitz dependence on state and uniform linear growth, with constants L and K; time dependence need only be Borel. Initially suppose E|ξ|² is finite. Each iterate is continuous and adapted, and growth bounds ensure square integrability. Itô isometry and Doob’s maximal estimate turn the integral error into a time integral of coefficient errors.

\[ |b(t,x)-b(t,y)|+\|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)\|\le L|x-y|,\qquad |b(t,x)|^2+\|\sigma(t,x)\|^2\le K(1+|x|^2). \]

\[ D_{n+1}(t):=E\sup_{u\le t}|X^{n+1}_u-X^n_u|^2\le C_T\int_0^tD_n(s)ds,\qquad D_{n+1}(T)\le A_T\frac{(C_TT)^n}{n!}. \]

The factorial is decisive. Even the square roots form a summable series, so successive maximal errors have an integrable sum. Paths converge uniformly almost surely and in the L² maximal norm. The limit is continuous and adapted. Cauchy–Schwarz passes the drift to the limit and isometry passes the noise, establishing the equation. [Companion proof 1](../foundations/t-sde-wellposedness.md#sde-proof-picard) starts with the moment bound for the first iterate and gives every step.

## 35.4 Uniqueness Means That an Error Cannot Create Itself

<a id="sde-stability"></a>

Subtract two solutions driven by the same noise. The same estimate now has no initial approximation error: present discrepancy is bounded by the integral of earlier discrepancy. With zero initial discrepancy, Gronwall forces it to vanish. Different initial values yield quantitative continuous dependence.

\[ E\sup_{s\le T}|X^x_s-X^y_s|^2\le C_T|x-y|^2,\qquad E\sup_{s\le T}|X_s|^2\le C_T(1+E|\xi|^2). \]

Gronwall here is explicit: iterating a nonnegative bound f(t)≤a+C∫₀ᵗf gives the first n terms of the exponential series and a remainder suppressed by (Ct)ⁿ/n!, hence f(t)≤aeᶜᵗ. Candidates known only to be locally integrable are first stopped at a common amplitude. Releasing the stops proves pathwise uniqueness beyond the L² class used for construction.

A finite initial variable need not have a second moment. Construct on the increasing time-zero events |ξ|≤n. Uniqueness makes the constructions agree on overlaps. These are initial-information events, not conditioning on future path events.

## 35.5 The Lifetime of a Local Rule

<a id="sde-explosion"></a>

Global Lipschitz bounds compare all positions, whereas many natural coefficients only satisfy them on bounded regions. Cut the coefficients off smoothly outside a ball. Each cutoff equation has a global solution, and two such solutions agree until leaving the smaller ball. They therefore glue into a maximal path.

\[ \tau_R=\inf\{t:|X_t|\ge R\},\qquad \zeta=\lim_{R\to\infty}\tau_R,\qquad \zeta<\infty\ \Longrightarrow\ \limsup_{t\uparrow\zeta}|X_t|=\infty. \]

The last implication uses boundedness and uniform local Lipschitz bounds on compact time-state regions. If the path remained in a ball before ζ, a cutoff solution would extend it continuously, contradicting maximality. Filling in an arbitrary finite value after explosion does not solve the original equation. A cemetery convention must restrict the equation to its lifetime.

Linear growth excludes explosion but is not necessary. More flexibly, find a nonnegative C² function V tending to infinity with |x| and satisfying LV≤C(1+V). Apply the stopped Itô formula, take expectations and use Gronwall to bound exit probabilities.

\[ P(\tau_R\le T)\le\frac{C_T(1+EV(\xi))}{\inf_{|x|=R}V(x)}\longrightarrow0,\qquad L_tV=b(t,x)\cdot\nabla V+\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\sigma\sigma^\top D^2V). \]

Add a constant to V if needed to make the denominator positive, and truncate general initial variables on time-zero events. This criterion controls escape to infinity; agreement of two solutions with the same noise is a different question. [Proof 2](../foundations/t-sde-wellposedness.md#sde-proof-local) handles gluing and stopped expectations.

## 35.6 A Jump Does Not Invalidate the Construction

<a id="sde-jumps"></a>

Chapter 34’s equations also include jumps. Let N be a Poisson random measure independent of W with deterministic compensator dtν(dz). Split marks into E₀ and E₁, with finite mass on E₁. Use a square-integrable compensated integral on E₀ and actual jumps on E₁.

\[ \begin{aligned}X_t={}&\xi+\int_0^tb(s,X_{s-})ds+\int_0^t\sigma(s,X_{s-})dW_s\\&+\int_0^t\!\int_{E_0}g(s,X_{s-},z)\widetilde N(ds,dz)+\int_0^t\!\int_{E_1}h(s,X_{s-},z)N(ds,dz).\end{aligned} \]

Require a Lipschitz bound on the combined squared differences of b, σ and g, integrating the latter over ν. Without the large jumps, compensated isometry and Doob give the same Picard estimate and a unique càdlàg strong solution. The condition is locally finite ∫∫|g|²νds, not merely predictability.

\[ |b(t,x)-b(t,y)|^2+\|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)\|^2+\int_{E_0}|g(t,x,z)-g(t,y,z)|^2\nu(dz)\le L^2|x-y|^2. \]

At the first E₁ arrival update x to x+h(t,x,z) and restart the small-jump equation. There are finitely many such jumps on a finite horizon, so interlacing is legitimate. The large-jump map need only be measurable and finite at each actual mark. Without a second-moment assumption we assert path existence, not a finite population second moment. Exact growth assumptions and restarting at random times appear in [proof 3](../foundations/t-sde-wellposedness.md#sde-proof-jump).

## 35.7 Lipschitz Is a Reliable Bridge, Not the Only One

<a id="sde-holder"></a>

A square-root velocity with no noise permits arbitrary waiting, but this does not prove that square-root noise causes nonuniqueness. Drift and diffusion enter the difference equation differently: drift contributes a first-order difference, diffusion enters through quadratic variation.

In one dimension let drift be locally Lipschitz and let the diffusion modulus satisfy |σ(t,x)−σ(t,y)|≤ρ(|x−y|), with ρ nondecreasing, vanishing at zero and positive elsewhere. The condition controlling the difference is

\[ \int_{0+}\frac{du}{\rho(u)^2}=\infty. \]

This allows ρ(u)=C√u. No unproved local-time theorem is needed. Approximate absolute value by smooth convex functions whose second derivatives lie in shrinking bands near zero and satisfy φₙ″(u)ρ(|u|)²≤2/n. Itô’s formula for the difference makes the second-order contribution vanish; drift leaves a Gronwall bound.

\[ E\phi_n(X_{t\wedge\tau}-Y_{t\wedge\tau})\le L\int_0^tE|X_{s\wedge\tau}-Y_{s\wedge\tau}|ds+\frac{t}{n}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad X=Y. \]

Localize both paths together. Keep the indicator of the stopping interval inside the drift integral before bounding it. A statement that non-Lipschitz coefficients work too would conceal the replacement mechanism. [Proof 4](../foundations/t-sde-wellposedness.md#sde-proof-modulus) constructs the functions and checks the limits.

## 35.8 Constructing the Law Before Choosing Its Driver

<a id="sde-weak-existence"></a>

Picard needs a usable bound on coefficient differences. With merely continuous coefficients, approximations need not be Cauchy on the same noise space. We can still freeze the current state over short intervals to generate Euler paths. Their laws may have convergent subsequences even when the original paths do not converge.

For bounded continuous time-homogeneous b and σ, drift bounds and the established continuous-martingale estimate bound fourth increment moments by C|t−s|². Chapter 24 gives tightness in continuous-path space. Itô martingale identities survive the limit because test functions and their generators are bounded continuous and the frozen state approaches the current state. The limit first gives a path probability, not a strong solution for a prescribed W.

We must still prove that this path probability can be supplied with a Brownian driver. That requires an exact formulation of the surviving identities. Weak existence is not simply another name for tightness.

## 35.9 An Equation That Does Not Name the Noise

<a id="sde-martingale-problem"></a>

If X solves the SDE, Itô’s formula separates drift from a conditionally fair remainder for smooth compactly supported f. This can be tested using only the law of X. Put a=σσᵀ, define the local generator, and require the following process to be a local martingale in the coordinate filtration of continuous-path space.

\[ Lf(x)=b(x)\cdot\nabla f(x)+\frac12\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}f(x),\qquad f\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^d),\qquad f(X_t)-f(X_0)-\int_0^tLf(X_s)ds. \]

This is our continuous diffusion martingale problem. It does not prescribe the driving Brownian path; it specifies conditional drift for every smooth observable. It connects with Chapter 28’s operators, but writing L is still not existence or uniqueness. We seek a path measure under which all these identities hold together.

Use compactly supported coordinate and coordinate-product cutoffs, stopped before leaving a ball. The identities reveal a continuous local martingale M with covariance ∫a(X)ds. Thus the martingale problem preserves more than average drift: it retains the entire local covariance of continuous noise.

\[ M_t=X_t-X_0-\int_0^tb(X_s)ds,\qquad [M^i,M^j]_t=\int_0^ta_{ij}(X_s)ds. \]

## 35.10 Recovering Noise Even from Degenerate Covariance

<a id="sde-reconstruction"></a>

The matrix σ need not be invertible and can even vanish. Writing σ⁻¹dM would then be meaningless. Invert only the directions through which σ transmits noise and fill unobserved directions with an independent Brownian motion.

\[ \sigma^+=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma^\top(a+n^{-1}I)^{-1},\quad P=\sigma^+\sigma,\quad W=\int\sigma^+(X_s)dM_s+\int(I-P(X_s))d\overline W_s. \]

The pseudoinverse is pointwise finite and Borel. Though it can be unbounded, its integral energy is tr(P)dt≤m dt, making the martingale integral legitimate. The two terms have combined bracket tI and zero cross bracket. Chapter 33’s Lévy characterization identifies W as Brownian in the enlarged filtration. The bracket of M−∫σdW then vanishes, giving the original equation.

Independent enlargement does not change the path law of X. This proves equivalence of the martingale problem and weak SDE under locally bounded Borel coefficients, continuous paths and the stated local integral conditions. It is not a proof of reverse realization for general jump generators. The [complete construction](../foundations/t-sde-structure.md#sde-proof-mp) also finishes the Euler weak-existence argument.

## 35.11 A Brownian Law Need Not Be Recoverable from a Given Noise

<a id="sde-tanaka"></a>

Weak existence without strong existence is a real distinction. Let σ(x) be 1 for x≥0 and −1 for x<0, and consider dX=σ(X)dW from zero. Every weak solution has bracket t, so X is Brownian and unique in law. Conversely take Brownian B and put W=∫σ(B)dB to construct a weak solution.

But changing the sign of the entire B path leaves W unchanged. Brownian motion spends zero Lebesgue time at zero, directly by its fixed-time Gaussian distribution and Tonelli. Hence σ(−B)d(−B)=σ(B)dB. The joint law is invariant under replacing (X,W) by (−X,W).

If X were determined by past W, then for every positive t both E[Xₜ|W]=Xₜ and E[Xₜ|W]=0 would hold, contradicting its Brownian variance. Joint-law uniqueness also needs justification: reintegrating gives W=∫σ(X)dX, determined by probability limits of simple approximations under the Wiener law of X. The joint law therefore does not depend on the original construction. [Proof 6](../foundations/t-sde-wellposedness.md#sde-proof-tanaka) supplies the measurable-integral step.

## 35.12 Putting Two Conditional Laws over the Same Noise

<a id="sde-yamada"></a>

In the Tanaka example, the noise leaves a choice of paths. If pathwise uniqueness removes that choice, does weak existence yield strong existence? We must legitimately compare weak solutions originally living on different spaces. An arbitrary coupling could reveal the future; Chapter 10’s conditional kernels must preserve temporal information.

Fix an initial law μ and disintegrate on standard Borel path spaces to obtain the conditional kernel K(Y,dx), where Y=(ξ,W). The input law is μ times Wiener measure. Draw two outputs independently from K given the same Y. The crucial test is that an output read only up to t has a conditional law depending only on ξ and W up to t, not on future increments.

\[ \Pi(dy,dx_1,dx_2)=\nu(dy)K(y,dx_1)K(y,dx_2),\qquad \nu=\mu\otimes\mathsf W. \]

This causal property follows from independence of Brownian future increments from the full past in the original weak solution. Verify it for past cylinder events and extend by a monotone class. On the coupled space W remains Brownian relative to both output pasts, and both integral equations remain valid. Pathwise uniqueness then identifies the outputs.

## 35.13 A Conditional Law Collapses to a Point

<a id="sde-yamada-conclusion"></a>

If two independent samples from a probability kernel always agree, the kernel cannot put positive mass in two distinct regions. A countable separating class shows that K is almost everywhere a point mass δF(Y). Its point is measurable in Y, and causality at rational times makes F(Y) adapted to the usual augmented input filtration. We may therefore set X=F(ξ,W) for any input with the prescribed law.

Cross-couple the kernels of two different weak solutions. Pathwise uniqueness forces them to select the same point. This proves the Yamada–Watanabe result used here: for continuous Brownian SDEs with deterministic Borel coefficients, within the class of nonexplosive weak solutions with a fixed initial law, weak existence and pathwise uniqueness imply strong existence and uniqueness in law. [Companion proof 2](../foundations/t-sde-structure.md#sde-proof-yw) handles transfer of the integral identity and completion of filtrations.

It does not say that any weak solution unique in law must be strong; the preceding example disproves that. Yamada and Watanabe’s 1971 paper studied this relationship. Our proof reconstructs it with conditional kernels and compatible information, using modern measurable language rather than claiming to reproduce the original exposition.

## 35.14 Why Square-Root Noise Has a Different Boundary

<a id="sde-sqrt"></a>

For dX=√|X|dW from zero, the zero process is a solution and the modulus condition makes it unique. There is no arbitrary waiting followed by spontaneous departure as with square-root velocity. The same square root in dt and dW has different uniqueness consequences because diffusion error is measured through quadratic variation.

More generally, continuous diffusion coefficients with linear growth first give weak existence. Under the one-dimensional modulus condition, Yamada–Watanabe upgrades it to strong existence and uniqueness. The linear-growth version follows from bounded cutoffs, uniform moment bounds and stopped tightness, as proved in the companion, rather than silently removing boundedness from a theorem.

## 35.15 How a Unique Law Produces the Markov Property

<a id="sde-markov"></a>

For time-homogeneous globally Lipschitz coefficients, solutions from different initial states give a measurable transition kernel. Rewrite the equation after a deterministic time s. The continuation uses only Xₛ and Wₛ₊ₜ−Wₛ. The latter is independent of the past, and pathwise uniqueness identifies the continuation with a restarted solution.

\[ E[f(X_{s+t})\mid\mathcal F_s]=P_tf(X_s),\qquad P_{s+t}=P_sP_t. \]

Restarting at a stopping time S requires Brownian strong Markov and the stopping identity for integrals; replacing s by S typographically is not a proof. Approximate bounded S from the right by grid-valued stopping times and use stability and continuity. Truncate general finite S. [Proof 4](../foundations/t-sde-structure.md#sde-proof-markov) gives measurability, Feller continuity and strong Markov.

Well-posed martingale problems also yield strong Markov, but measurable selection and stopped conditional laws need proof. We establish that route for globally bounded continuous coefficients with weak existence and uniqueness in law from every state; the globally Lipschitz linear-growth case uses uniform moment localization. We do not extend it to arbitrary operators and state spaces without hypotheses.

## 35.16 Moving All Initial States Together

<a id="sde-flow"></a>

Existence for each x does not automatically give a continuous random map for all x outside one common null set. Initial states are uncountable; pointwise probability-one statements cannot simply be intersected. This is the first extra responsibility of a stochastic flow.

Take time-homogeneous b,σ∈Cᵦ³: the functions and derivatives through order three are bounded and continuous. BDG and Gronwall give pth maximal moment estimates for spatial differences, for arbitrary p≥2. Taking p above the parameter dimension lets the Kolmogorov grid argument yield a jointly continuous version in (s,t,x). High moments here establish regularity of a random parameter field, not existence of a single SDE.

\[ \Phi_{s,t}(x)=x+\int_s^tb(\Phi_{s,u}(x))du+\sum_{k=1}^m\int_s^t\sigma_k(\Phi_{s,u}(x))dW^k_u,\qquad \Phi_{s,t}=\Phi_{u,t}\circ\Phi_{s,u}. \]

The identity first holds at countably many rational parameters by pathwise uniqueness, then extends through the joint continuous version. The flow relation thus holds simultaneously for all deterministic s≤u≤t and x, rather than with a newly chosen exceptional set each time.

## 35.17 The Jacobian Satisfies Its Own Stochastic Equation

<a id="sde-jacobian"></a>

An initial-state difference quotient suggests a new equation: the linearized equation along the solution. First derivatives propagate perturbations, second derivatives control the remainder, and high moments with parameter continuity yield a spatial C¹ version.

\[ dJ_{s,t}=Db(\Phi_{s,t})J_{s,t}dt+\sum_kD\sigma_k(\Phi_{s,t})J_{s,t}dW_t^k,\qquad J_{s,s}=I,\qquad J_{s,t}=D_x\Phi_{s,t}. \]

Construct a linear inverse matrix K as well. The product rule requires an extra drift ∑K(Dσₖ)² to make d(KJ)=0. Thus J is everywhere nonsingular and each Φₛ,ₜ is a local C¹ diffeomorphism. Nonsingularity alone does not prove global surjectivity: a uniform bound must prevent distant initial states from entering bounded regions, after which fiber cardinality stays one along time. This global step and the common null set are supplied in [Proof 5](../foundations/t-sde-structure.md#sde-proof-flow) states exactly the established flow scope and this boundary.

We can now observe how the same noise stretches, compresses and continuously transports a collection of initial states. This does not grant arbitrary locally Lipschitz coefficients a simultaneous smooth structure, and strong solution must not be confused with spatial smoothness.

## 35.18 Small Noise Does Not Eliminate Rare Large Deviations

<a id="sde-small-noise"></a>

Multiplying noise by ε makes solutions typically approach deterministic motion by stability estimates. Chapter 29 distinguished typical closeness from rare large deviations. With state-dependent feedback, can Brownian path large deviations be transferred to the solution?

\[ dX_t^\varepsilon=b(X_t^\varepsilon)dt+\varepsilon\sigma(X_t^\varepsilon)dW_t,\qquad X_0^\varepsilon=x. \]

We cannot simply treat a stochastic integral as a continuous function of the Brownian path in the uniform topology and invoke contraction. Left integrals carry second-order information; the Itô solution map does not have the naive continuity needed. Freeze coefficients on each interval to obtain a continuous Euler map and prove exponential approximation to the true solution.

## 35.19 Transmitting Cost through a Controlled Equation

<a id="sde-fw"></a>

We prove the following Freidlin–Wentzell version: finite T, fixed initial state and globally bounded Lipschitz b and σ, allowing degeneracy. In the uniform topology of C([0,T],Rᵈ), Xε satisfies a good LDP with speed ε⁻² and rate

\[ I_x(\phi)=\inf\left\{\frac12\int_0^T|u_s|^2ds:\ \phi_t=x+\int_0^tb(\phi_s)ds+\int_0^t\sigma(\phi_s)u_sds\right\},\qquad \inf\varnothing=\infty. \]

The quantity minimized is control energy needed to generate the path, not an inverse σ⁻¹ written without checking invertibility. For a fixed mesh, Schilder and contraction from Chapter 29 give Euler path costs. Bounded-energy controls yield uniformly bounded, equicontinuous paths, and controlled Euler solutions converge uniformly to controlled solutions.

The hardest step is stochastic approximation error, not guessing the rate formula. Apply log(ρ²+|e|²) to the error. Lipschitz bounds control its drift and martingale bracket. An exponential martingale bound and short-window Brownian oscillation give superexponential error, first as ε tends to zero and then as the mesh vanishes. The order matters. The [complete proof](../foundations/t-sde-small-noise.md#sde-proof-fw) supplies this estimate and transfer of open and closed bounds.

## 35.20 The Same Rule, the Same Law and the Same Path

<a id="sde-insight"></a>

The waiting parabolas show that local rules can leave a choice. Explosion shows that absence of choice does not guarantee unlimited lifetime. Tanaka’s equation is subtler: the motion has a unique law, yet a given noise does not determine it. Yamada–Watanabe shows that a legitimate weak construction and absence of choice for the same input collapse the conditional law into a measurable output.

An equation as a rule, a process as an existing object, a law as a measure on path space, and the ability of input to determine output are separate provable claims. Lipschitz theory makes them hold together, inviting their confusion. Flows and control energy show what becomes possible afterward: transporting initial states together and assigning costs to rare paths.

Even when a one-dimensional equation has a unique solution, its boundary hitting, escape and long-term location need not be apparent. Existence and uniqueness provide the object to study, not its fate. The next useful task is to find scales that turn competition between drift and fluctuation into computable quantities.
