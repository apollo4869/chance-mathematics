# Chapter 56: How Can Random Structures Split, Merge and Remain Consistent?

Deleting an unsampled leaf from a tree should not suddenly change when the remaining individuals share an ancestor. Yet separate, seemingly reasonable branching rules for samples of one hundred or one thousand individuals need not have that property. Each finite model may be valid without describing a common population.

A static tree has distances and mass. When it breaks or which classes merge are additional rules. The test is not whether its animation looks coherent. After some labels are hidden, do the remaining observations still obey the prescribed probability law? That requirement constrains local rates, infinite-process construction and the allocation of mass to fragments.

## 56.1 A seemingly fair balanced split fails first

<a id="fragment-finite-failure"></a>

Let each block split at rate 1, dividing its observed m individuals as evenly as possible and choosing uniformly among such divisions. Two individuals always separate, at rate 1. Among three split into 1 and 2, a specified pair separates with probability only 2/3. Even after the third individual is deleted from the record, that individual has changed the pair’s waiting law.

\[ r_2=1,\qquad r_{3\to2}=\frac23,\qquad
r_{3\to2}\ne r_2. \]

The finite chains themselves run perfectly well. The error is treating balance by observed sample size as sampling from one population. If the population splits into two equal masses, each individual’s side is random; the pair separates with probability 1/2, and the sample need not be balanced. We need laws compatible with deletion of labels.

## 56.2 Record ancestry before inserting a clock

<a id="hierarchy-space"></a>

A partition says who belongs together at one instant. A hierarchy also records which classes lie within larger classes. A hierarchy H on [n] contains the empty set, [n] and every singleton; two members are either disjoint or nested. This excludes having both {1,2} and {2,3} as family branches, since they overlap without nesting.

\[ r_mH=\{A\cap[m]:A\in H\},\qquad
r_mH_n=H_m\quad(m\le n). \]

Restriction does not discard every class containing an unobserved label; it takes intersections. Define an infinite hierarchy by compatible finite restrictions. Finite hierarchy spaces are finite discrete spaces; their inverse limit is compact Polish with its Borel field generated by finite evaluations. Consistent finite probabilities give a unique infinite law by Chapter 8’s countable construction and a countable intersection of compatibility events. Cylinder extension gives exchangeability. See [T-HIERARCHY-CONSISTENCY](../foundations/t-hierarchy-consistency.md#hierarchy-projective).

## 56.3 A finite hierarchy is a tree, but not yet a metric tree

<a id="hierarchy-finite-tree-main"></a>

Make every nonempty class a vertex and connect it to its smallest strict containing class. Nesting makes that parent unique, strict inclusion excludes cycles and singletons are leaves. Conversely descendant-label sets recover the hierarchy. This is a bijection, not merely a tree-shaped picture.

Restriction deletes leaves and suppresses unary nodes. A three-leaf tree with unit edges may lose an edge after retaining two leaves; assigning fresh unit lengths changes distances. A consistent hierarchy supplies neither consistent edge lengths nor the order of splits on different branches.

## 56.4 Frequencies at each level still miss their linkage

<a id="hierarchy-linkage"></a>

Consider two levels: parent masses (.6,.4) and child masses (.4,.3,.2,.1). One arrangement puts .4,.2 in the first parent and .3,.1 in the second. Another puts .3,.2,.1 in the first and .4 alone in the second. Ranked frequencies at each level agree, but the probabilities that three samples share a parent and have three distinct children are 0 and .036.

\[ P_A(\text{same parent, three children})=0,\qquad
P_B(\text{same parent, three children})=6(.3)(.2)(.1)=.036. \]

Chapter 47’s paintbox identifies one exchangeable partition. What is missing here is how the same samples enter every level simultaneously. Independently drawing paintboxes at each level does not make them nest.

## 56.5 A nested paintbox comes from de Finetti on whole rows

<a id="hierarchy-nested-main"></a>

For jointly exchangeable refining partitions Π⁽ᵐ⁾, independently label every block at every level by a continuous color. Record all colors of individual i as one row Xᵢ∈[0,1]ᴺ. The rows are exchangeable, so Chapter 43’s standard Borel de Finetti theorem supplies a random directing probability Q. Entire rows are conditionally iid; levels are not independent.

\[ i\sim_{\Pi^{(m)}}j\iff X_i(m)=X_j(m),\qquad
Q^{\otimes2}\{x_m=y_m,\ x_\ell\ne y_\ell\}=0\quad(\ell<m). \]

The countable condition expresses nesting and suffices for the converse construction. Noncollision, measurable conditional kernels, common null sets and frequency identification are proved in the [companion](../foundations/t-hierarchy-consistency.md#hierarchy-nested-paintbox). The gain is not another explanation of one-level mixtures: one Q preserves random parent-child linkage.

## 56.6 Tree representation must state its dust boundary

<a id="hierarchy-tree-boundary"></a>

If each specified finite level is dust-free, positive colors are countable and every child has a unique parent. Put level m at height 1−2⁻ᵐ, connect by segments and complete. Pushing Q forward through full addresses gives a measured real tree; it is compact when every finite level has finitely many positive blocks. The scoped representation includes proofs of unique arcs, measurable mass pushforward and fringe identification.

If the first level is entirely dust, this fixed-height construction puts distinct samples at distance 2, impossible as an iid distance law of a separable probability space by Chapter 55. More general hierarchy representations require different height and mass structures; the dust-free theorem is not a general classification. Even with representation, the hierarchy does not identify lengths: putting all mass at the leaf of a root edge of length 1 or 2 gives the same sampled classes but different root distances.

## 56.7 Fragmentation means more than refining classes

<a id="fragment-branching"></a>

A refining path may be driven by a common hidden clock, making every branch wait together. Refinement and exchangeability alone do not give independent fragment futures. We additionally require Markov behavior and, conditional on the current state, independent evolution of blocks according to the same relative rule. That is the branching fragmentation property. Homogeneous models preserve the clock of small blocks; self-similar models specify how mass changes it.

\[ X^{(x)}(t)\ \overset d=\
\operatorname{rank}\{x_i X_j^{(i)}(t):i,j\ge1\}
\quad\text{in the homogeneous case}. \]

This identity alone does not guarantee existence. A construction must specify relative dislocations and check the event rates visible to finite samples.

## 56.8 Why a dislocation measure need not be a probability

<a id="fragment-dislocation"></a>

\[ \mathcal S^\downarrow=\{s_1\ge s_2\ge\cdots\ge0:\sum_i s_i\le1\},
\qquad
\nu\{(1,0,\ldots)\}=0,\qquad
\int(1-s_1)\nu(ds)<\infty. \]

The vector s records positive relative masses after a split and 1−Σsᵢ is newly produced dust. The measure ν records rates per operational time and need not have finite total mass: tiny changes may occur infinitely often. The trivial vector (1,0,…) is excluded because ineffective events cannot be identified from the process. The integrability condition ensures finite observations are not changed infinitely often.

\[ P(\text{a split separates }m\text{ samples}\mid s)
=1-\sum_i s_i^m\le m(1-s_1),\qquad m\ge2. \]

This estimate explains the role of 1−s₁. It controls distinguishable jumps, not the total number of events. For example place unit mass at each vector (1−2⁻ᵏ,2⁻ᵏ,0,…). Total ν-mass is infinite but the weighted integral is finite, so every finite observation can still have a legitimate path.

## 56.9 An infinite homogeneous process from compatible Poisson atoms

<a id="fragment-poisson"></a>

\[ \kappa(d\pi)=\int PB_s(d\pi)\nu(ds)
+c\sum_{i\ge1}\delta_{\varepsilon_i}(d\pi),\qquad
N(dt,d\pi,dk)\ \text{has intensity }dt\,\kappa(d\pi)\,\#(dk). \]

PB is the established paintbox kernel. The partition εᵢ isolates member i while keeping the rest together; c≥0 is erosion. Enumerate members within each current block and use π to refine it. Order global blocks by least label; k selects the current block. On [n], only k≤n and atoms separating the first n internal ranks can matter, with bounded rate. Run finite restrictions first and verify compatibility using shared atoms, rather than forcing an infinite-activity process to have a next global jump.

\[ \lambda_m=mc+\int(1-\sum_i s_i^m)\nu(ds)<\infty
\quad(m\ge2),\qquad
L_N(f\circ r_n)=(L_nf)\circ r_n. \]

Intertwining gives semigroup and path consistency, invariance of κ gives exchangeability and separated block-generator terms give independent futures. T-HOMOGENEOUS-FRAGMENTATION proves these steps, càdlàg partition paths and the strong Markov property. For m=1 the effective rate is 0; that formula must not be used with m=1.

## 56.10 Erosion produces dust without positive children

<a id="fragment-erosion"></a>

Keep erosion alone, ν=0. Each label is isolated at rate c. Surviving labels have frequency e⁻ᶜᵗ and still form one block; all others are singletons. The largest block shrinks continuously, with no positive children receiving its eroded mass.

\[ X(t)=(e^{-ct},0,\ldots),\qquad D(t)=1-e^{-ct}. \]

Labels have not disappeared from N. Each dust singleton has frequency 0 while their union can have positive frequency. Asymptotic frequency is not a countably additive probability on N, so infinitely many zeros cannot simply be added. In paintbox language the same phenomenon is realized by nonatomic mass.

## 56.11 Frequencies must be chosen jointly over time

<a id="fragment-frequencies"></a>

Quoting Chapter 47 separately at each time does not define a mass path. Fix label i and observe its first separation times τᵢⱼ from the others. They are exchangeable in the other labels and de Finetti makes them conditionally iid. The uniform empirical-distribution strong law supplies limits for every threshold t simultaneously, defining all tagged block masses on one common good set.

\[ |B_i(t)|=\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac{\#(B_i(t)\cap[n])}{n}
=M_i((t,\infty]),\qquad\text{simultaneously for all }i,t. \]

These paths are nonincreasing and right-continuous. A zero-frequency block containing two people would persist to a later rational time by right continuity, contradicting the rational-time paintbox result. Ranked partial sums are nonincreasing and right-continuous, giving càdlàg mass paths in the coordinate topology of S↓. Coordinate convergence has not been replaced by ℓ¹ convergence.

## 56.12 A typical tag does not follow the largest block

<a id="fragment-tag"></a>

A typical tag enters child i with probability sᵢ and may enter dust. This size bias changes sampling weights, not the fragmentation mechanism. Let Zₜ be its block mass. Taking minus its logarithm turns successive multiplicative changes into additive jumps.

\[ \begin{aligned}
Z_t&\overset d=e^{-\xi_t},\\
E Z_t^q&=\exp\{-t\Phi(q)\},\qquad q>0,\\
\Phi(q)&=c(q+1)+\int(1-\sum_i s_i^{q+1})\nu(ds).
\end{aligned} \]

The subordinator ξ has drift c and killing rate c+∫(1−Σsᵢ)ν. Why does erosion contribute twice? Isolating the tag itself kills it; while the tag survives its whole block still shrinks, giving drift. The proof first computes integer moments from the exponential probability that q+1 labels have not separated, then uses Bernstein uniqueness to identify the actual mass law. Logarithmic intuition has not replaced construction. The jump measure, integrability bound and joint time law are in the [complete proof](../foundations/t-homogeneous-fragmentation.md#homfrag-tag).

## 56.13 An exact conservation criterion for homogeneous models

<a id="fragment-conservation"></a>

\[ E\sum_iX_i(t)
=\exp\!\left\{-t\left[c+\int(1-\sum_i s_i)\nu(ds)\right]\right\}. \]

Let q decrease to 0 in the tagged mass moment and apply monotone convergence. Since total mass is nonincreasing and at most 1, all-time conservation holds precisely when c=0 and Σsᵢ=1 for ν-almost every s. First use expectation 1 at countably many rational times, then monotonicity for all times. An expectation identity was not silently promoted to a simultaneous path assertion.

This criterion concerns homogeneous clocks. Changing small-fragment clocks can compress infinitely many conservative splits into finite time. That issue must now be examined afresh.

## 56.14 Self-similarity prescribes how mass changes time

<a id="fragment-selfsimilar"></a>

\[ X^{(m)}(t)\overset d=mX^{(1)}(m^\alpha t),
\qquad m>0. \]

This is a precise scaling assumption. Starting from mass m multiplies child masses by m and speeds time by m^α. Small blocks are slower for α>0 and faster for α<0; α=0 is homogeneous. Neither exchangeability nor conservation implies it.

To avoid hiding an unfinished general time change in one sentence, we fully construct finite-ν models with c≥0 and arbitrary real α. Infinite-ν self-similar construction needs additional block-dependent stopping-line control and is not claimed as proved here.

## 56.15 Operational and physical time for one fragment

<a id="fragment-clock"></a>

\[ A_m(u)=\int_0^u(me^{-cr})^{-\alpha}\,dr,\qquad
\text{split rate at current mass }x:\quad\lambda x^\alpha,
\quad \lambda=\nu(\mathcal S^\downarrow). \]

In operational time a fragment born with mass m erodes as m e⁻ᶜᵘ and splits after Exp(λ), using ν/λ. Invert A to obtain physical time. Differentiation gives x′=−c x^(α+1) and split rate λx^α. The identity Aₘ=m⁻ᵅA₁ explains self-similarity directly. Reversing the exponent would reverse which small fragments are fast.

Generate independent environments at countably many genealogical vertices. Use half-open child intervals for mass and iid uniform tags in the same [0,1). Membership, mass and sampling consistency then live on one probability space. Finite projections need not be Markov because they do not reveal true block masses. The infinite state, including frequencies, is sufficient for the future.

## 56.16 Explosion still requires specifying the tags

<a id="fragment-explosion"></a>

At most 1/ε blocks have mass at least ε, and their total split rate is uniformly bounded. A lineage staying above ε cannot split infinitely often in finite time. Ancestral jumps accumulating in finite time must therefore have masses tending to 0. The intersection of its nested intervals contains at most one point, so two distinct uniform tags almost surely cannot both remain there. Such tags become singleton dust rather than a ghost zero-mass block containing several individuals.

T-SELFSIMILAR-FRAGMENTATION proves generation truncation, finite-rate bounds, limiting paths and conditional futures. A sample of n still has at most n−1 visible refinements even when the population has infinite activity or shattering. These meanings of explosion must be kept separate.

## 56.17 Every split may conserve mass while all mass becomes dust

<a id="fragment-shattering"></a>

\[ \nu=\delta_{(1/2,1/2,0,\ldots)},\quad c=0,\quad\alpha<0,
\qquad
\zeta_{\rm tag}=\sum_{k\ge0}2^{\alpha k}E_k<\infty\ \text{a.s.} \]

Along a typical tag the mass after k splits is 2⁻ᵏ and its next wait is 2^(αk) times an independent Exp(1). Their expectations have finite sum, so infinitely many waits fit into finite time almost surely. More strongly, the maximum Mₖ among 2ᵏ exponential waits at generation k satisfies EMₖ≤1+k log 2. The almost surely finite sum Z=Σ2^(αk)Mₖ bounds every lineage lifetime. After the finite random time Z all positive blocks have vanished.

Every local dislocation sums to 1; loss comes from infinite accumulation. With α≥0, finite ν, c=0 and Σsᵢ=1 for ν-almost every dislocation, a typical tag’s physical waits dominate a divergent iid exponential sum, giving conservation. This is why the homogeneous conservation criterion cannot simply be transferred to negative indices.

## 56.18 Exchangeability, conservation and self-similarity are three conditions

<a id="fragment-three-conditions"></a>

Changing the unit split to (1/3,1/3,0,…) leaves the homogeneous process exchangeable and self-similar but loses one third at every split. Changing equal-binary split rates to r(x)=1+x leaves exchangeability and conservation, but not power-law self-similarity: r(1/2)/r(1)=.75 whereas r(1/4)/r(1)=.625 is not .75².

Specifically isolating label 1 while keeping all others together preserves total positive mass 1, but the three-label pattern {1}|{2,3} privileges a name and is not exchangeable. These counterexamples concern distinct assumptions. None replaces the others.

## 56.19 Generator relations make clock differences calculable

<a id="fragment-generators"></a>

\[ \begin{aligned}
F_q(x)&=\sum_i x_i^{q+1},\\
\mathcal A_\alpha F_q(x)&=-\Phi(q)\sum_i x_i^{q+1+\alpha},
\qquad q>0,\quad q+\alpha\ge0
\quad(\nu\ \text{finite}).
\end{aligned} \]

Compute the first-event and erosion derivative from one block, then use independent branching. The difference quotient is bounded by a constant times Σxᵢ^(q+1+α), at most a constant under the stated condition, justifying differentiation under the infinite sum. For α=0, Fq is a semigroup eigen-observable: PₜFq=e⁻ᵗΦ⁽ᑫ⁾Fq. Starting from unit mass, eᵗΦ⁽ᑫ⁾Fq(Xₜ) is a mean-one nonnegative martingale, directly by conditional semigroup expectations, without retelling Chapter 20’s population story.

Changing α makes the derivative involve a different mass power; the mean evolution no longer closes into the same exponential formula. A self-similar clock changes each mass’s contribution to future evolution, rather than merely speeding an animation.

## 56.20 What is lost when a path is reduced to a hierarchy

<a id="fragment-path-hierarchy"></a>

Collect the blocks appearing at all rational times of a refining process and add singletons and the root. This gives an exchangeable hierarchy. Each finite restriction has finitely many effective changes, and right continuity ensures rational observations omit no finitely visible classes. This is an actual map from dynamics to a static hierarchy.

The inverse is not unique. On one genealogical environment, multiplying all times by 2 or changing mass-dependent clocks may preserve parent-child classes while changing waits. A hierarchy has not selected Markov behavior, independent branching or α for us.

## 56.21 Merger rates must also pass the deletion test

<a id="coalescent-rates"></a>

\[ \lambda_{b,k}=\lambda_{b+1,k}+\lambda_{b+1,k+1},
\qquad2\le k\le b. \]

With b current blocks, λ is the rate at which a specified k-tuple merges. Adding one invisible block allows the original tuple to merge alone or with that block. Both give the same restricted event, so rates must add. Kingman pair rate 1 satisfies the relation; choosing one uniformly random pair at total system rate 1 does not, since a specified pair then has rate 1/3 with three blocks but rate 1 with two.

## 56.22 Why consistent single-group merger rates produce Λ

<a id="coalescent-measure"></a>

\[ \lambda_{b,k}=\int_{[0,1]}
x^{k-2}(1-x)^{b-k}\Lambda(dx),\qquad
\Lambda\ \text{finite},\quad0^0=1. \]

This is more than guessing an integral satisfying recursion. Put aₙ=λₙ₊₂,₂. Recursion makes all alternating differences nonnegative. Use those differences as binomial weights on j/m to construct finite measures of total mass a₀. Their factorial moments equal aᵣ and ordinary-moment errors vanish. Compactness supplies a limit, Bernstein approximation proves uniqueness, and y↦1−x yields Λ. T-LAMBDA-COALESCENT proves this inverse argument, giving necessity and sufficiency in the precise class of single-group merger jumps.

## 56.23 Many blocks may merge while finite observations remain locally finite

<a id="coalescent-poisson"></a>

For x>0 generate events at intensity dt x⁻²Λ(dx). Independently mark each current block with probability x and merge all marked blocks. Among b visible blocks the probability of at least two marks is at most binom(b,2)x², cancelling x⁻² and bounding effective finite rates. The atom Λ{0} is supplied separately by Kingman pair clocks. Shared uniforms and pair clocks construct all restrictions on one space, proving measurability, exchangeability, càdlàg paths and projection consistency.

If Λ{0}=a>0, the finite-chain Lyapunov sum f(b)=Σⱼ₌ₘ₊₁ᵇ1/[a binom(j,2)] bounds the expected first time below m by 2/(am). Thus P(N(t)>m)≤2/(amt), giving coming down from infinity. But Λ=δ₁ leaves infinitely many singletons until its first Exp(1). General Λ models do not automatically share Kingman’s short- or long-time behavior.

## 56.24 Dust can enter positive blocks under coagulation

<a id="coalescent-dust-main"></a>

\[ \Lambda\{0\}=0,\quad
\beta=\int_{(0,1]}x^{-1}\Lambda(dx)<\infty
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
D(t)=\prod_{u\le t}(1-x_u),\quad ED(t)=e^{-\beta t}. \]

Conditional on the event x values, an original singleton remains unselected with the displayed product probability. Independent marks across labels and the strong law give the frequency of unselected tags. At its first selection a tag joins a positive fraction of other unselected tags in a positive block. For \(\Lambda=\delta_{1/2}\), dust is 2⁻ᴷ⁽ᵗ⁾ with K a rate 4 Poisson count.

This differs from erosion under fragmentation. Dust is not nothing: it retains labels not belonging to positive-frequency blocks, which can form positive blocks at global merger events. The finite-β assumption is essential to this argument. It does not automatically prove the general dust equivalence or the full criterion for coming down from infinity.

## 56.25 Why reversing the film does not automatically give fragmentation

<a id="fragment-reversal"></a>

Two labels suffice. Starting forward from singletons, merge at rate 1 with E∼Exp(1). Reverse on a fixed [0,T]. Conditional on currently being merged, the reverse splitting rate is

\[ r_{\rm rev}(u)=
\frac{e^{-(T-u)}}{1-e^{-(T-u)}},\qquad0\le u<T. \]

Divide P(T−u−h<E≤T−u) by hP(E≤T−u) and let h decrease to 0. The rate depends on time and diverges at the terminal point, so it is not homogeneous fragmentation with fixed ν. Special dualities exist in specified models but require initial laws, time changes and proofs, not simply opposite arrows.

## 56.26 Cutting an actual tree starts with finite spans

<a id="fragment-geometric-cuts"></a>

On a compact real tree with mass μ, put length measure ℓ on the skeleton and Poisson cuts with intensity dt ℓ(dz). A CRT may have infinite total length, so finitely many whole-tree cuts cannot be expected in finite time. But the span of n samples and the root has finite length and sees locally finite cuts.

\[ i\sim_tj\iff [x_i,x_j]\text{ contains no cut before }t,
\qquad P(i\sim_tj\mid T,x_i,x_j)=e^{-t\,d(x_i,x_j)}. \]

The real-tree inclusion [x,z]⊂[x,y]∪[y,z] makes this an equivalence relation. Shared cuts and iid mass samples give exchangeability, nesting and sampling consistency. T-TREE-CUTS constructs sigma-finite length through countably many added ancestral segments and builds a measurable conditional Poisson kernel on a fixed coding-parameter space.

## 56.27 One geometry permits different dynamics; the interval is explicit

<a id="fragment-cut-model"></a>

On the unit interval with Lebesgue mass, an interval of length x is cut at rate x at a uniform conditional location, and future cuts in distinct intervals are independent. This is exactly the α=1, c=0 fragmentation whose ν is the law of ranked (U,1−U). Finitely many cuts occur in finite time and length is conserved. This fully derives dynamic parameters from geometry in a specified model.

On the same CRT, using a bounded nonnegative Borel weight w(z)ℓ(dz), or no cuts, gives pair-separation rates ∫[x,y]w dℓ or 0. Recording only component masses may discard geometry controlling the future. Independent cuts do not automatically identify a particular mass-fragmentation law. The special Brownian CRT index, conditional component laws and additive-coalescent duality are not proved here.

## 56.28 Theoretical scope and checkable sources

<a id="fragment-sources"></a>

The working proofs cover finite hierarchies and compatible infinite construction; nested paintboxes at countably many specified levels and dust-free measured-tree representation; homogeneous fragmentation for general integrable dislocation measures with erosion, tagging and conservation; all-real-index finite-ν self-similar processes and shattering; single-group Λ rate classification and construction with the stated dust/CDI results; and Poisson cutting of compact real trees and measurably coded random trees.

The full representation classification of arbitrary unclocked exchangeable hierarchies, the inverse decomposition of general homogeneous splitting measures, infinite-ν self-similar stopping-line theory, simultaneous multi-group Ξ mergers and special CRT cutting dualities are not proved by these results, nor borrowed from Chapters 57–58. Scope is part of a theorem and cannot be filled by a similar name.

Separate history from modern reconstruction. The balanced-split failure, clock calculations and order of counterexamples here are a modern mathematical organization, not a reconstruction of historical thought processes.

## 56.29 Consistency turns local rules into one random world

<a id="fragment-ending"></a>

We can now separate three claims that are easily conflated. A hierarchy records containment. Splitting or merger rates specify when it changes. Sampling consistency ensures different sample sizes observe one evolution. None proves the others.

Dust makes the distinction concrete. Labels can all remain while total positive mass decreases. Conservative local splits may accumulate under accelerated clocks into all dust. Conversely mergers can turn individually zero-frequency singletons into positive blocks. No abstract symmetry has selected time direction or conservation laws.

A static random tree is not a unique film waiting to be played. We can impose checkable cutting rules and know that they still need consistency and mass tests. If instead we add one branch at a time while keeping old and new finite observations compatible, which local growth rules can genuinely reconstruct random geometry? That requires a new construction, not merely reversing today’s splitting arrows.
