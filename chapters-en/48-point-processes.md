# Chapter 48: When a Random Outcome Is a Collection of Points

A device leaves two locations in [0,1] on every trial. Follow either location separately and its long record is uniform. Someone therefore proposes that two independent uniform draws already describe the device.

A second device instead draws one uniform U and puts its two locations at U and 1−U. It too always leaves two points, each separately uniform, but retains a constraint absent from the independent model. What is missing from the count and the individual marginals when describing the whole collection?

## 48.1 Two records with the same total

<a id="points-count-location"></a>

Observe only the left half A=[0,1/2). In the independent model both points lie on the right with probability 1/4, so A can be empty. In the mirror model A has exactly one point except on the null event U=1/2, and is never empty. Yet both models have expected count 2|D| in every Borel region D and total count identically two. What is missing really is spatial dependence, not an inaccurate measurement of the total.

Chapter21 treated arrival times, and Chapters27,34,37 used particular Poisson random measures. The new task is not another Poisson series calculation: arbitrary locations, random counts, multiplicities and limits must coexist in one legitimate state space.

## 48.2 One unit of mass for each occurrence

<a id="points-state"></a>

\[ \xi=\sum_j m_j\delta_{x_j},\qquad \xi(A)=\sum_jm_j\mathbf1_{\{x_j\in A\}},\quad m_j\in\{1,2,\ldots\}. \]

The xⱼ are distinct and mⱼ records how many occurrences occupy that location. Relabelling leaves ξ unchanged, and regional counts are ξ(A). Two approaching points can form 2δ₀, so configurations with no coincidences are not sufficient for limits. A point measure is simple if every singleton has mass at most one. Simplicity is an additional property, not the meaning of a point measure.

Throughout this chapter E is a locally compact second-countable metric space, hence standard Borel, with compact exhaustion Kₘ⊂int Kₘ₊₁. Examples include Rᵈ, open regions and countable discrete spaces. Require finite total multiplicity on every compact set: local finiteness. Such a measure is Radon; their space is Mₚ(E). Conversely, every locally finite integer-valued Radon measure has this atomic form. The [complete proof](../foundations/t-point-measures.md#point-space-atoms) uses integer values and a countable base to exclude hidden non-atomic mass.

## 48.3 Window boundaries are part of the model

<a id="points-local"></a>

The measure \(\sum_{k\ge1}\delta_{1/k}\) is not locally finite on the real line because [0,1] contains infinitely many points. On the open space (0,1), omitting k=1 which lies outside, it is locally finite: every compact stays a positive distance from zero. The same drawing can be legitimate or illegitimate depending on the state space.

Local finiteness requires neither finitely many points in all of space nor finite expected local counts. A finite random intensity with infinite mean will later still produce finite counts in compact windows. Properties of individual configurations and of their mean measure must be kept separate.

## 48.4 Observations must first be measurable

<a id="points-evaluation"></a>

Use the sigma-field generated by all evaluations ξ↦ξ(A), with A Borel. A point process Ξ is a measurable random element of this space. The word process does not require a time index: one outcome is the whole spatial collection. For nonnegative measurable f, the observation Ξf is measurable by increasing simple approximation.

Marking sometimes requires selecting every occurrence measurably, including repeated occurrences at one location. Partition the space into countably many finite-count pieces and sort by a standard-Borel real code. Counts of codes at most rational q recover the jth code. Finite sorting selects genuine positions, allowing kernel randomisation to mark occurrences independently. The [enumeration proof](../foundations/t-point-measures.md#point-space-measurable) also shows why this auxiliary numbering does not become part of the final state.

## 48.5 Softening the edges of a window

<a id="points-tests"></a>

Moving a point across a window boundary makes a hard count jump. To study nearby configurations, first give positions continuous weights f∈\(C_c(E)\) and read ξf. Compact support restricts the observation to a finite window; continuity avoids artificial boundary jumps. Define vague convergence by convergence of all such test integrals.

This choice has consequences. On the real line δₙ converges vaguely to zero although its total count is always one, while \(\delta_{1/n}+\delta_{-1/n}\) converges to 2δ₀, retaining multiplicity. The topology does not see an individual point escaping to infinity but does see two occurrences merging at a finite location.

## 48.6 Countable tests support the entire state space

<a id="points-topology"></a>

Choose countably many uniformly dense continuous tests on compact windows and include nonnegative compactly supported χₘ≥1 on Kₘ. This gives a countable nonnegative family (fⱼ) and the following metric. Integrals against χₘ control local counts, so coordinate convergence extends by uniform approximation to every compactly supported continuous test.

\[ d(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{j\ge1}2^{-j}(1\wedge|\xi f_j-\eta f_j|). \]

Bounded counts in each window allow diagonal extraction of the tests, producing a positive linear functional. [T-RIESZ](../foundations/t-riesz.md) actually represents it by a Radon measure. Counts on small balls with zero boundary mass are integer limits, so the limiting measure is still a point measure. This proves relative compactness under local count bounds and completeness of the metric. Finite configurations on a countable dense set give separability. Thus Mₚ is Polish, and its Borel sigma-field equals the evaluation sigma-field. [T-POINT-MEASURES](../foundations/t-point-measures.md) proves all steps. We establish only the structure needed for integer measures, without borrowing Chapter49.

## 48.7 A reading that retains joint information

<a id="points-laplace"></a>

\[ L_\Xi(f)=\mathbb E\exp\{-\Xi f\},\qquad f\in C_c^+(E). \]

This is the Laplace functional. It lets all relevant positions participate while keeping the output in [0,1], without assuming finite expected counts. More importantly, taking f=Σtᵢfᵢ gives the whole joint Laplace transform of (Ξf₁,…,Ξfₖ), not just individual marginals.

Laplace transforms determine nonnegative vector laws: transform each coordinate Y to e⁻ʸ. Integer parameters give every mixed moment on the compact cube; the finite-product Bernstein argument from Chapter13 determines continuous tests, and the Borel inverse exponential recovers the vector. The countable deciding tests generate the sigma-field of Mₚ, so all L(f) determine its law uniquely. The [full proof](../foundations/t-point-laplace.md#point-laplace-unique) also explains why a countable rational cone suffices.

## 48.8 Poisson construction includes both counts and positions

<a id="points-poisson-construction"></a>

For finite intensity μ first choose K∼Poisson(μ(E)), then K independent locations of law μ/μ(E); zero intensity means the empty configuration. The Poisson count and iid locations jointly determine a new law; the mean intensity alone cannot imply it. For nonnegative measurable f, conditioning on K and summing the Poisson series gives the following expression directly.

\[ L_N(f)=\exp\left\{-\int_E(1-e^{-f(x)})\,\mu(dx)\right\}. \]

For sigma-finite intensity, partition the space into countably many finite-mass pieces, construct independently and add. Functionals multiply for finitely many pieces; bounded convergence on the left and monotone convergence in the exponent give the same formula for the whole configuration. Regional counts are measurable random sums. This construction works on a standard-Borel space, but belonging to Mₚ(E) additionally requires finite intensity on every compact window.

## 48.9 Independent scattering is not a single marginal

<a id="points-independent-scattering"></a>

Insert \(f=\sum_i t_i\mathbf1_{A_i}\) for disjoint finite-intensity sets. The exponent becomes Σμ(Aᵢ)(1−e⁻ᵗⁱ), so the joint transform factors into Poisson transforms. Uniqueness gives independent counts N(Aᵢ)∼Poisson(μ(Aᵢ)). This is independent scattering of a Poisson random measure.

Conversely, independent-scattering counts determine the functional for simple tests and then for all nonnegative tests by monotone approximation. This is a two-way characterisation. A Poisson count in one window, or specified separate marginals, cannot replace the proof of joint independence.

## 48.10 Infinite intensity is not the same as coincident points

<a id="points-intensity-boundary"></a>

Locally finite Radon μ gives EN(Kₘ)=μ(Kₘ)<∞; on one common full-probability set all these counts are finite. If μ(K)=∞ for a compact K, then Eexp(−tN(K))=0 for every t>0, forcing N(K)=∞ almost surely. The sigma-finite intensity \(\mathbf1_{(0,1)}(x)\,dx/x\) on the real line puts infinitely many points inside [0,1].

By contrast μ=aδ₀ with finite a places a finite Poisson multiplicity at zero. It is locally finite but need not be simple. A locally finite Poisson process is simple almost surely exactly when μ is atomless: atomless sampling has no collisions among countably many occurrences, while a positive intensity atom has positive probability of count at least two. [T-POISSON-SPACE](../foundations/t-poisson-space.md#poisson-space-local) proves both directions.

## 48.11 Space retains a law after conditioning on the total

<a id="points-finite-window"></a>

If μ(A)=λ∈(0,∞), the restriction of N to A has the law obtained by choosing a Poisson(λ) count and iid locations of law μ(·∩A)/λ: their functionals agree for every nonnegative test on A. Hence given N(A)=k, the configuration is the unordered measure of k iid draws. This conditions on a positive-probability count event and identifies the whole conditional configuration law; it does not divide by a probability-zero location event.

For example, if disjoint regions A,B each have mean count one and independent counts, the probability both are empty is e⁻². Knowing only that each region is empty with probability e⁻¹ does not specify this joint probability. Spatial models contain precisely such joint information.

## 48.12 A shared environment creates spatial dependence

<a id="points-cox"></a>

Let Λ be an evaluation-measurable random locally finite Radon measure. Constructing Poisson(Λ) conditionally on Λ gives a Cox process. The conditioning must actually be implemented: on fixed window pieces, measurable Poisson inverse CDF sampling uses Λ(Dₘ), and measurable randomisation uses the location kernel Λ(·∩Dₘ)/Λ(Dₘ), with zero mass producing no points. Countably many independent uniforms complete the construction. The [proof](../foundations/t-poisson-space.md#poisson-space-cox) supplies the kernel rather than merely postulating it.

\[ L_N(f)=\mathbb E e^{-\Lambda(1-e^{-f})},\qquad \operatorname{Cov}(N(A),N(B))=\operatorname{Var}(Z)\mu(A)\mu(B)\quad(\Lambda=Z\mu,\ A\cap B=\varnothing). \]

The covariance identity assumes EZ²<∞ and finite μ-mass of both regions. It follows from conditional independence and conditional mean Zμ(A). Nondegenerate Z leaves positive correlation, so a Cox process generally is not Poisson with intensity EΛ. If Z is finite almost surely but EZ=∞, each local configuration is still finite while the mean intensity may be infinite on every positive-μ window.

## 48.13 Marks belong to occurrences, not just positions

<a id="points-marking"></a>

Each arrival may carry a size, type or direction. Given a standard-Borel mark space F and kernel κ(x,dy), mark each occurrence independently conditionally on N. Two occurrences at one location require two draws. The count in a ground window A×F remains N(A). If F also has a locally compact second-countable topology, the marked measure is locally finite on the product.

\[ L_M(f)=\exp\left\{-\int_E\int_F(1-e^{-f(x,y)})\kappa(x,dy)\mu(dx)\right\}. \]

Condition on N. Independent marking makes the conditional expectation of exp(−Mf) the product of κ(x,e⁻ᶠ) over occurrences. Write it as exp{−N[−log κ(x,e⁻ᶠ)]} and insert the proved Poisson functional for nonnegative measurable tests. The result identifies the entire PRM of intensity μ(dx)κ(x,dy), not just expected mark counts.

## 48.14 After moving points, check local counts again

<a id="points-mapping"></a>

For measurable T:E→F the image count is \(T_*N(A)=N(T^{-1}A)\). If the image intensity \(T_*\mu\) is finite on compacts in F, substituting f∘T proves the image process is Poisson(\(T_*\mu\)). The condition cannot be omitted: mapping every point of a Lebesgue-intensity real-line process to zero produces infinitely many occurrences in one compact singleton.

Even a legitimate image may not be simple. Collapsing a finite-intensity interval to zero creates a finite Poisson multiplicity. For arbitrary point processes a continuous proper map guarantees pathwise local finiteness through compact preimages; for Poisson it suffices to check intensity preimages. Marking with a random displacement and then mapping gives a displacement theorem, with the same check required.

## 48.15 When deletion leaves two independent processes

<a id="points-thinning"></a>

Use marks 0 and 1, retaining an occurrence at x with probability q(x), independently across occurrences conditionally on the original process. For tests f,g of retained and discarded processes, the marked functional splits into two exponentials. Thus they are independent with intensities qμ and (1−q)μ. Chapter21 finite-type time thinning becomes a theorem for spatially varying kernels.

Countably many independent PRMs can likewise be superposed if the summed intensity is locally finite, by multiplying functionals and taking monotone limits. Retaining only the leftmost point in a finite window gives a count at most one, generally not Poisson. Marginal retention probabilities cannot replace conditional independence of the deletion decisions.

## 48.16 Averaging contributions from a collection of points

<a id="points-campbell"></a>

\[ \nu(A)=\mathbb E\Xi(A),\qquad \mathbb E\sum_{x\text{ occurrence in }\Xi}f(x)=\int f\,d\nu\quad(f\ge0). \]

This is Campbell’s mean formula for any point process, not only Poisson. For nonnegative simple functions it is linearity of expected counts; monotone convergence gives general f. Tonelli makes ν a measure, but it need not be locally finite. Signed f requires ∫|f|dν<∞.

If a point’s reward depends on the whole configuration, say f(x,Ξ), this formula cannot simply be reused. Observing from a selected existing point needs the Palm or Mecke structure of Chapter50. A deterministic mean formula does not already establish that conditional probability. The two initial models with equal means also show why correct Campbell calculations do not identify the law.

## 48.17 Compensation makes second moments explicit

<a id="points-compensation"></a>

\[ I_1(f)=\int f\,d(N-\mu),\qquad \mathbb EI_1(f)=0,\quad \mathbb E[I_1(f)I_1(g)]=\int fg\,d\mu. \]

Start with simple functions on disjoint finite-intensity cells. Independence eliminates cross terms and the Poisson variance equals its mean, giving the isometry. Approximate every L²(μ) kernel by finite-window simple functions and extend by L² completeness. This is a spatial integral with deterministic kernels. For merely L², not L¹, functions it is not subtraction of two possibly infinite ordinary integrals. Chapter34 predictable time integration does not authorise arbitrary random spatial kernels.

## 48.18 Why second-order contributions are not just squares

<a id="points-multiple"></a>

To record joint contributions from two distinct occurrences, squaring a centred count reuses the same occurrence. For K=N(A), λ=μ(A), the appropriate second-order polynomial is C₂=(K−λ)²−K, rather than (K−λ)² alone. Poisson moment calculations give mean zero, variance 2λ², and orthogonality to K−λ.

\[ e^{-\lambda t}(1+t)^K=\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{t^r}{r!}C_r(K,\lambda),\qquad \mathbb EC_rC_s=\mathbf1_{\{r=s\}}r!\lambda^r. \]

The generating identity extends the distinct-occurrence correction to every order. The expected product of two generating functions is exp(λst), giving orthogonality by coefficient comparison. Define Iᵣ on finite disjoint partitions using products of these polynomials. Refinement is consistent by the binomial identity obtained from multiplying generating functions. This yields E[Iᵣ(f)Iᵣ(g)]=r!〈f,g〉 for symmetric L² kernels, followed by extension through density and completeness.

The construction works even with atoms in μ. Deleting the entire spatial diagonal would be wrong because distinct occurrences may share a location. [T-POISSON-CHAOS](../foundations/t-poisson-chaos.md) proves the complete orthogonal expansion, refinement consistency and the selected L² decoupling identity: assigning r coordinates to r independent noise copies gives Jᵣ with E|Iᵣ(f)|²=r!E|Jᵣ(f)|². This is a norm equality, not equality in law or a general Lᵖ tail theorem. A first reading can follow the repeated-occurrence correction before entering the full construction.

## 48.19 What local-count tightness excludes

<a id="points-tightness"></a>

For Mₚ-valued Ξₙ, their laws are tight exactly when counts on every Kₘ are uniformly tight. In one direction choose integers aₘ with exceedance probabilities at most ε2⁻ᵐ. Configurations satisfying every bound have compact closure and probability at least 1−ε. Conversely, continuous χₘ integrals are bounded on a probability-tight compact family and control its window counts.

Uniform bounds on expected local counts are convenient sufficient conditions, not necessary ones. The finite heavy-tailed random intensity above may have infinite local means, while a single point-process law on the Polish space remains tight. An expectation bound is a method, not the definition of tightness.

## 48.20 Turning a functional limit into a process limit

<a id="points-convergence"></a>

Theorem: if Ξ is an Mₚ-valued point process, Ξₙ⇒Ξ is equivalent to Lₙ(f)→L(f) for every f∈\(C_c^+\). Necessity is bounded continuous testing. Sufficiency still needs tightness. For χₘ≥1 on Kₘ use the following estimate.

\[ \mathbb P\{\Xi_n(K_m)>R\}\le\frac{1-L_n(a\chi_m)}{1-e^{-aR}}\quad(a>0). \]

First make a small so the limiting numerator approaches zero; then choose R large, and handle the finitely many initial n separately. This yields uniform local-count tightness. Chapter24 Prokhorov gives weak subsequential limits. Every limit has the same Laplace readings, so uniqueness identifies it with Ξ. Identification and tightness are distinct parts of the proof, not an unexplained assertion that transform convergence implies process convergence.

## 48.21 Rare counts become rare locations

<a id="points-rare-limit"></a>

Independent experiment j places a point with probability pₙⱼ and conditional location law Qₙⱼ, otherwise none. Suppose νₙ=ΣpₙⱼQₙⱼ converges vaguely to locally finite μ and maxⱼpₙⱼQₙⱼ(K)→0 for every compact K. The finite-row superposition Ξₙ then converges to Poisson(μ).

\[ a_{nj}=p_{nj}Q_{nj}(1-e^{-f}),\qquad \log L_n(f)=\sum_j\log(1-a_{nj})=-\sum_ja_{nj}+o(1)\longrightarrow-\mu(1-e^{-f}). \]

Within the support window max a tends to zero and the sum stays bounded, so the sum of squares is bounded by their product and vanishes. This controls the entire logarithmic remainder. The preceding theorem yields process convergence. The new result concerns joint spatial tests, rather than only the total-count limit of Chapter13. If all rare experiments share one trigger and place n points together when triggered, the limit may instead be empty despite a fixed total mean. Independence rules out precisely such clustered triggering.

## 48.22 Three limits: boundaries, collisions and escape

<a id="points-counterexamples"></a>

Under vague convergence to ξ, direct count convergence ξₙ(A)→ξ(A) requires relatively compact A with ξ(∂A)=0. For example \(\delta_{1/n}\)→δ₀, while counts in (0,1) remain one for n≥2 and the limiting count is zero; the boundary carries mass. The random version requires P{Ξ(∂A)=0}=1 before applying the continuous mapping theorem.

Simple configurations can converge to configurations with multiplicities, and locally finite configurations can move all mass outside every fixed window. The first requires multiplicities in the state space; the second shows that vague limits do not control total counts. These identify what the observations retain. A problem requiring preservation of global counts needs an additional no-escape condition or a stronger topology.

## 48.23 Names are forgotten; locations remain

<a id="points-insight"></a>

The original two-point record is now a random element of Mₚ. A varying count is no longer an obstacle: a counting measure retains positions and multiplicities in one object, countable tests make it measurable, the Laplace functional identifies its law, and local-count control proves limits. Each layer solves a different problem; a scatterplot cannot replace them.

What we forget is auxiliary numbering, not locations or dependence induced by a shared intensity. Poisson and Cox models, and equal-intensity examples with different counts, distinguish mean density, joint law and conditional independence. Multiple integrals further distinguish coincident positions from reuse of the same occurrence. These distinctions are testable in formulas, not merely alternative wording.

Unit mass remains a restriction. If an experiment leaves a continuous mass or a random probability distribution instead of integer occurrences, the integer-limit argument no longer applies. Continuous tests still make sense. The next task is to determine which measurability, tightness and identification mechanisms survive when mass is no longer assembled from individual points.

## 48.24 Sources, proofs and scope

<a id="points-sources"></a>

Cox’s [1955 original paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1955.tb00188.x) concerns statistical methods for event series. We do not attribute every spatial point-process structure to one historical discovery. The construction and organisation here are a modern reconstruction. Last–Penrose’s [author manuscript](https://stoch.math.kit.edu/img/Last/lastpenrose2017.pdf) provides a systematic reference for checking the scope of Poisson functionals, marking and orthogonal expansions; the proofs here are supplied rather than replaced by citations.

The complete proofs are [T-RIESZ](../foundations/t-riesz.md), [T-POINT-MEASURES](../foundations/t-point-measures.md), [T-POINT-LAPLACE](../foundations/t-point-laplace.md), [T-POISSON-SPACE](../foundations/t-poisson-space.md), [T-POINT-TRANSFORMS](../foundations/t-point-transforms.md), and [T-POISSON-CHAOS](../foundations/t-poisson-chaos.md). Scope is locally finite point processes on the specified locally compact spaces, PRMs with sigma-finite standard-Borel intensities, evaluation-measurable locally finite Cox models, and deterministic-kernel L² multiple integrals. General random-Radon topology belongs to Chapter49; Palm, Mecke and Gibbs to Chapter50. General Lᵖ decoupling, non-sigma-finite PRMs and arbitrary non-locally-compact topologies are not proved here.
